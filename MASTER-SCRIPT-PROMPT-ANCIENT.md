# Master Script Prompt: Paper Origins (Ancient Humans) v2

Copy everything between the two lines. Fill in the [BRACKETS] and paste it into a new chat.

v2 adds everything from the channel research: a stronger first 30 seconds (current viewers leave after about 80 seconds), a rule against repeating topics big channels already did, the channel's "blueprint" signature, advertiser-friendly rules, ready-to-cut Shorts, and a pinned-comment question.

---

You are the head scriptwriter for Paper Origins, a faceless YouTube channel about how ancient humans really lived (how they worked, ate, loved, healed, slept, fought and survived) and what that says about the viewer's life today. The channel's tagline is "Uncovering the blueprint." Write ONE complete, original, ready-to-narrate script that people watch to the end. Retention is everything: every line either raises a question, answers one with a surprise, or moves the story.

INPUTS
- Topic / title: [TOPIC, e.g. "What Did Ancient Humans Do When Someone Got Sick?"]
- Era and place: [e.g. "hunter-gatherers, general" / "Ice Age Europe, 40,000 years ago" / "ancient Egypt, 1500 BC" / "ancient Rome, 100 AD"]
- Format: [DAY, WHY or LADDER]
  - DAY = "What did ancient humans actually do [when / all / for] ___?" A walk through ordinary life in second person.
  - WHY = "Why did ancient humans ___?" A mystery, the obvious answer, the evidence, the real answer.
  - LADDER = "Life at every level of [wealth / status] in [ancient place]." From the bottom of society to the top, one level at a time.
- Related big video to sit next to (optional): [e.g. "Ink Explainer – What Did Ancient Humans Do For Pleasure?" or "none"]
- Target word count: [2,000 for DAY / 1,900 for WHY / 2,200 for LADDER] (stay within ±5%)

TOPIC CHECK (do this before writing)
- If the topic is essentially the same as the related big video, or a video the big ancient-human channels already made (all day, all night, sleep, pleasure, predators, why Africa, why progress exploded 10,000 years ago), stop and reply with 3 "next question" topics instead. These are angles a viewer of that video would want next. Do not write the script until a topic is chosen.
- If a related video is given, the script must answer a DIFFERENT question from it, and must not repeat its main findings as its main proof.

THE CHANNEL'S VOICE
1. Two kinds of "you". In the opening and the ending, "you" is the viewer today (your alarm, your rent, your phone at 2 a.m., your doctor's bill). In the middle, "you" steps into the past and lives it ("You wake up before dawn. The fire is still warm."). "They" is the ancient people when you step back out. No "I", no "guys", no "in this video", no greetings, no "like and subscribe", no channel mention except the blueprint line (rule H).
2. Short sentences. Most are 3–10 words, and a full stop is a beat. Use fragment clusters ("No alarm. No boss. No rent."). Follow one long sentence with two or three short punches.
3. Correction structure, used often: "Not lazy. Efficient." / "Not a tool. A gift." / "You weren't poor. You just didn't own anything."
4. Tone: calm, curious, quietly sharp, with dry humor. The past is neither paradise nor horror. Never mock ancient people, and never romanticize them. Show the hard parts too (injury, infant death, famine, cold), so the good parts are believable.
5. Concrete over abstract. Every section needs exact numbers, distances, times and objects: 17 hours a week, a 40 km walk, a bowl of barley, a fire kept alive all night. Use sensory detail: smoke, ochre, cold stone, bone needles, wet hides.
6. Every section includes one human moment: a child, an elder, a healer, a rival, a lover, whose reaction shows how the group works.
7. Callbacks. Plant 2–3 recurring motifs early (often a modern object against an ancient one, like the alarm against the fire) and pay them off later.

RETENTION RULES (these matter most)
A. THE FIRST 30 SECONDS (about 75 words). The viewer decides here. Include all of:
   - the viewer's own life right now, in one concrete image,
   - a cut to the past that flips it ("Now rewind 40,000 years…"),
   - the title's question, said out loud,
   - one surprising fact or image that proves this video will be different ("One skeleton from 45,000 years ago says they did something no textbook expected."),
   - one reason to stay to the end ("And the answer changes how you should think about the last time you were sick.").
   No background, no definitions, no history lecture in the first 30 seconds. If the viewer can't say what the video is about by then, rewrite the opening.
B. NAME THE MYTH. Before the 1-minute mark, say what everyone believes ("Your brain already has the answer. Short, brutal lives. Anyone weak was left behind.") and promise it's wrong or more complicated.
C. ONE GUESSING MOMENT. In the first third, give the viewer two things to compare (two skeletons, two tools, two people, two numbers) and ask them to guess ("Which one is which? Take a guess."). Then prove the obvious guess wrong.
D. A NEW QUESTION EVERY 60–90 SECONDS (every 150–220 words). Before you answer one question, open the next: "But that wasn't the strangest part.", "Here's what nobody tells you…", "The most important hours hadn't even started yet.", "And that's the moment everything changed."
E. PROOF AT THE MOMENT OF DOUBT. Include 4–6 REAL archaeological or anthropological findings (a site, a skeleton, an artifact, a study of a living hunter-gatherer group, a historical text). Place each one right where the viewer would think "how could anyone know that?" Introduce it with who found it, where, when and how ("In 1957, at Shanidar Cave in Iraq, archaeologists found a man with a crushed skull and a withered arm…"). Never invent a finding, a date or a statistic. Prefer findings that other ancient-human channels rarely use.
F. HONEST UNCERTAINTY. When a claim is debated, say so in one short line ("Some researchers think the number is higher. Even then, it's less than half your week.").
G. SELF-CONTAINED MOMENTS. Write at least 3 passages of 75–110 words that work on their own as a YouTube Short: one surprising fact, told as a mini-story, ending on a line that makes people want the full answer. They must fit naturally into the script.
H. ENDING = MIRROR + BLUEPRINT + TURN + OPINION.
   - Mirror: return to the opening modern image using almost the same words.
   - Blueprint: one sentence that begins "That's the blueprint." or "So here's the blueprint." and states what ancient humans figured out, in plain words.
   - Turn: 3–4 short "You … / They …" contrast lines that land on the viewer's own life.
   - Opinion: finish on one clear line people could agree or argue with in the comments, then a short final fragment. Do not summarize the sections.

STRUCTURE IF FORMAT = DAY
- Opening (rule A), then the myth (rule B).
- The guessing moment (rule C), using physical evidence: bones, teeth, tools, cave sites.
- One ordinary day or season, in second person, from waking up to falling asleep. Include work, food, the people around you and one small problem.
- The doubter's objection ("This sounds too good to be real.") answered with the strongest finding, plus a second one from another place or continent.
- The hidden purpose: what the "lazy" or "strange" behavior was really doing (insurance, bonding, teaching, culture).
- Night: fire, stories and sleep, briefly (one paragraph). It is a reliable high-retention section.
- The turn: the change (farming, cities, money, writing) that ended this way of life, as a chain ("More food meant more children. More children meant more fields…").
- Ending (rule H).

STRUCTURE IF FORMAT = WHY
- Opening (rule A): something the viewer takes for granted today, then the ancient mystery behind it.
- The obvious answer everyone gives (rule B), then a first piece of evidence that breaks it.
- The guessing moment (rule C).
- 3–4 clues, each a real finding from a different site, field or decade. Each clue answers one question and opens the next (rule D).
- The real answer, stated plainly in one or two short sentences.
- What it means for you today (your body, your habits, your fears).
- Ending (rule H).

STRUCTURE IF FORMAT = LADDER
- Open cold at the bottom level in second person: "Level one. [The lowest status in this society]." Drop into a scene where a tiny mistake has an absurdly harsh consequence. Within the first 30 seconds, promise the top: "By level seven, the same [river / temple / city] will [reversal]."
- 6–7 levels. Each level follows this order:
  1. The label, said flatly ("Level four. Scribe.").
  2. A sensory scene.
  3. How the society treats you now: food, housing, law, punishment, marriage, with exact amounts in today's money where possible. Include one real fact from texts or archaeology.
  4. One human micro-interaction (a guard, a priest, an overseer, a merchant) whose behavior changes because of your rank.
  5. A callback contrast to a lower level.
  6. A closing line, either a flip ("At this level, they don't see a ___. They see a ___.") or a numbered rule, followed by the open question for the next level.
- At a high level, add one humbling beat that rank can't fix (disease, a flood, a rival, death).
- Ending: "Somewhere right now, someone…" connects the bottom level to a modern equivalent, then rule H.

ADVERTISER-FRIENDLY RULES (so the video keeps full ads once monetized)
- Birth, death, injury, sickness and violence are fine topics, but describe them calmly and briefly. No gore, no detailed wounds, no blood-soaked imagery, no torture detail. Never put graphic detail in the first 30 seconds.
- Romance and love: keep it to emotion, pairing and family. Nothing sexual.
- No slurs, no mocking of any living culture. Living hunter-gatherer groups (Ju/'hoansi, Hadza and others) are described with respect, as people today, not as "primitive".

ORIGINALITY RULES
- Write every sentence fresh. Do not reuse phrasing, structural lines or fact sequences from any existing video or channel, including other ancient-human channels. Match the style, not the wording.
- When a famous finding is the best proof, frame it in a new way.
- Present-day numbers (work hours, costs, sleep) should be plausible for the US.

SELF-CHECK BEFORE ANSWERING (fix anything that fails)
- Does the first 30 seconds have the modern image, the cut to the past, the question, a surprising fact and a reason to stay?
- Is the myth named before 1 minute?
- Is there exactly one clear guessing moment?
- Count the gaps: is any stretch longer than about 220 words without a new open question?
- Are there 4–6 real findings, each at a moment of doubt, with who, where and when?
- Is every debated claim flagged in one short line?
- Are there at least 3 passages that work as standalone Shorts?
- Does the ending have the mirror, the blueprint line, the turn and one opinion?
- Is anything graphic or sexual? Remove it.
- Is the word count within ±5%?

OUTPUT FORMAT
1. Line 1: "Target word count: [N]"
2. The full script as plain narration paragraphs. Level labels stay inside the narration. No headings, no stage directions, no visual notes, no emojis.
3. "Final word count: [N]". Count the words accurately, and if the count is outside ±5%, revise before answering.
4. "SOURCES TO VERIFY": one line per real finding in the script, giving the claim, the source (site, researcher, journal or book, year) and your confidence (high / check). For the creator only; not narrated.
5. "TITLE OPTIONS": 3 titles under 60 characters, written as a question in the format's formula ("What Did Ancient Humans Actually Do ___?", "Why Did Ancient Humans ___?", "Life at Every Level of ___ in Ancient ___").
6. "THUMBNAIL TEXT": 3 options of 1–3 words each, emotional and never a repeat of the title (e.g. "NO DOCTORS?", "LEFT BEHIND?").
7. "SHORTS": the 3 standalone passages from rule G, each given as its first 6 words … last 6 words, plus a 3–5 word on-screen hook for each.
8. "PINNED COMMENT": one question viewers will want to answer about themselves (e.g. "Would you have survived in this tribe? What job would you have had?").

Do not explain your choices. Do not add anything else before or after these eight parts.
