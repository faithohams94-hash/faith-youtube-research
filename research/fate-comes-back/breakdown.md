## Why it works (structure notes)

1. **Hook in about 90 seconds.** A public humiliation plus an unfair, concrete loss ("That's days of my wages"), then a status reveal. The viewer is committed before 5:00.
2. **"Would you have done it if they were rich?"** is the engine. Every Selena scene is the same test with a new victim: grandmother, Zara, Ruth, her own mother. Escalation comes from **who** she hurts, not how loudly.
3. **The heroine is never passive.** Zara refuses (the shoe), sets terms (the kiss), and asks for space (the alone day). The man rescues her, but she never begs.
4. **The second conflict is the "good" character.** After the villain is gone, the obstacle becomes Florence's loving manipulation. This keeps a romance with no villain left alive for 30 minutes, and gives a fresh "all is lost" that isn't another Selena attack.
5. **Merit firewall.** Abigail exists so the romance never cheapens the job. This is repeated four times (13:57, 53:19, 1:21:08, 1:37:00) because the audience will ask.
6. **Comedy beats every 2–4 minutes**: fake heart attack, 100 copies, the fence, ski "refrigerator with legs," the bouquet bigger than her. These lower the tension so the cruel scenes land harder.
7. **Callbacks do the emotional work:**
   - Printer 3 vs 100 → "I'm good at extra copies" → "She'll keep me employed longer than that printer"
   - Chair (wobbly chair, Ruth's chairs, "a chair, water, listen first," Esther's dry chair for young Noah)
   - Cocoa with salt (grief → planned by nobody)
   - Grandad's piano song (6 years of silence → Sunday breakfast)
   - "Delivery girl" (insult at 1:34 → reclaimed at 1:28:23)
   - Food (kicked chicken → Auntie feeds everyone)
8. **Redemption, not destruction.** The villain loses the man and her job, but gets an apology arc and a mirror good deed. Each apology is answered with "trust will take longer," which keeps it believable.
9. **The fate twist reframes the title.** The kindness that was humiliated in scene one (feeding a stranger) is the same kindness that once saved the hero as a child. It's planted subtly by Esther's "I know that face" at 31:16.
10. **Dialogue style.** Short lines, frequent comic understatement ("Not once have you asked about Abigail's gardening"), and moral lines given to the elders.

## Reusable formula (for an original story in this genre)

1. Kind, poor heroine with a loving guardian figure and a concrete job.
2. A disguised or overlooked elder is mistreated in public. The heroine defends them and loses something specific.
3. Reveal the elder's status, and that they are related to a rich, decent but emotionally absent man engaged to the abuser.
4. Hire, not rescue. Add a gatekeeper who protects the heroine's merit.
5. The abuser repeats the same sin on 3–4 escalating victims, the last being her own family.
6. Proof from a low-status witness, then the engagement ends at the midpoint.
7. A forced-proximity trip, engineered by the meddling elder.
8. A wound exchange: both leads share a parent-loss or parent-absence story.
9. Public confrontation in which the heroine reclaims the insult.
10. The meddling is exposed and the heroine asks for her own choice. She chooses him freely.
11. A fate twist linking an early kindness to the hero's past.
12. Redemption for the villain, a merit promotion for the heroine, and a shared meal at the heroine's home.
