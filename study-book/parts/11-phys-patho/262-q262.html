PART A — HICCUP (SINGULTUS)

Definition

Hiccup is an involuntary, intermittent, spasmodic contraction of the diaphragm (and external intercostal muscles) producing a sudden inspiration that is abruptly stopped about 35 milliseconds later by closure of the glottis. The inrushing air striking the closed glottis produces the characteristic "hic" sound. Rate: about 4–60 per minute.

Classification by duration
  1. Acute / transient (benign): lasts minutes to < 48 hours — very common.
  2. Persistent: > 48 hours.
  3. Intractable: > 1 month — usually indicates organic disease.
Mechanism — the hiccup reflex arc
  1. Receptors / afferent limb: irritation of sensory endings in the stomach, oesophagus, diaphragm, pharynx, pericardium or mediastinum, carried by the vagus nerve, the phrenic nerve (sensory fibres from diaphragm) and sympathetic afferents from T6–T12.
  2. Centre: a "hiccup centre" in the brainstem (medulla) close to the respiratory centre, receiving modulating input from the hypothalamus and higher centres (emotion, excitement). It is separate from the rhythmic respiratory generator — hiccups are not influenced by normal breathing rhythm but are inhibited by ↑ PaCO2.
  3. Efferent limb: (a) phrenic nerve (C3–C5) → sudden contraction of diaphragm (often one side, usually the left); (b) nerves to external intercostals → inspiratory effort; (c) recurrent laryngeal branch of the vagus → abrupt closure of glottis; accessory muscles may also join.
  4. Result: a sudden negative intrathoracic pressure and short inspiration that is instantly cut off by the closing glottis → "hic". Expiratory muscles are not involved.

Physiological basis of simple remedies
  1. Breath-holding / rebreathing into a bag: ↑ PaCO2 inhibits the hiccup centre.
  2. Valsalva manoeuvre, drinking ice-cold water, swallowing granulated sugar, pulling the tongue: stimulate pharyngeal/vagal afferents that interrupt the reflex.
  3. Pulling knees to chest: compresses the diaphragm. Intractable hiccups need treatment of the cause (± drugs acting centrally, e.g. chlorpromazine) — clinical level.

Significance: transient hiccups are harmless; persistent hiccups may cause exhaustion, insomnia, weight loss, aspiration and wound dehiscence after surgery, and point to serious disease. The fetus hiccups in utero; the reflex may be a vestige of the suckling/air-clearing reflex.

PART B — YAWNING

Definition

Yawning is an involuntary, stereotyped reflex consisting of wide opening of the mouth with a slow, deep inspiration, a brief pause at the peak (acme) with stretching of facial, jaw and neck muscles, followed by a relatively quick, passive expiration. A yawn lasts about 6 seconds and is often accompanied by eye closure, lacrimation, stretching of limbs (pandiculation) and transient hearing reduction (Eustachian tube opening).

Phases
1. Slow, deep inspiration + gaping of jaw→2. Acme: max. muscle stretch, eyes close→3. Quick expiration, muscles relax
Neural and neurochemical mechanism
  1. Centre: yawning is generated in the brainstem (near the respiratory and cranial-nerve motor nuclei — V, VII, IX–XII) under the control of the paraventricular nucleus (PVN) of the hypothalamus.
  2. Pro-yawning neurotransmitters: dopamine (D2 receptors) activates oxytocinergic neurons of the PVN → oxytocin acts on hippocampus/pons → cholinergic (ACh, muscarinic) output triggers the yawn. Serotonin, excitatory amino acids, nitric oxide and ACTH/α-MSH also induce yawning.
  3. Anti-yawning: opioids (enkephalins, morphine) inhibit yawning — so excessive yawning is a sign of opioid withdrawal.
  4. Motor output: trigeminal (jaw opening), facial, glossopharyngeal, vagus, hypoglossal, phrenic and intercostal nerves acting together.
Proposed functions (theories)
  1. Arousal / state-change theory: yawning occurs at transitions (before sleep, on waking, in boredom). The deep breath and muscle stretch ↑ venous return, heart rate and cerebral blood flow, and stretch receptors stimulate the reticular activating system → ↑ alertness.
  2. Brain-cooling (thermoregulatory) theory: the deep inhalation of cooler air and ↑ blood flow through the face and skull act as a heat exchanger to cool the brain.
  3. Respiratory (O2/CO2) theory: the old idea that yawning corrects hypoxia/hypercapnia; experiments breathing high CO2 or 100% O2 do not change yawn frequency, so this is not supported.
  4. Re-expansion of alveoli: the deep breath opens collapsed alveoli and spreads surfactant (similar to a sigh).
  5. Social / contagious yawning: seeing, hearing or reading about yawning triggers a yawn — linked to mirror neurons and empathy; reduced in autism and schizophrenia.
Pathological (excessive) yawning

Excessive yawning occurs in sleep deprivation and sleep disorders, opioid withdrawal, drugs (SSRIs, dopamine agonists), vasovagal reactions, brainstem stroke and raised intracranial pressure, multiple sclerosis, epilepsy (aura), and hepatic failure. Yawning is reduced in Parkinson's disease (↓ dopamine).

Conclusion

Hiccup and yawning are both brainstem respiratory reflexes. Hiccup is a purposeless myoclonus of the diaphragm cut short by glottic closure; it is usually benign but, if persistent, signals disease of the reflex arc. Yawning is a hypothalamus-driven reflex that promotes arousal and brain cooling; excess yawning may signal neurological or drug-related disorders.

Understand it (plain English) Think of hiccup as a faulty doorbell circuit: something irritates a wire (vagus or phrenic) — a full stomach, a hot pepper — and the brainstem fires the diaphragm and slams the "door" (glottis) shut at the same time. Every "hic" is air hitting a closed door. Holding your breath raises CO2, which damps the centre. Yawning is the opposite of slamming the door — the mouth and glottis open wide for a big slow breath. Your hypothalamus uses it to "reboot" alertness and cool the brain, which is why you yawn when bored, sleepy or when someone else yawns.
Memory trick Hiccup arc nerves: "VPS" in (Vagus, Phrenic, Sympathetic T6–T12) and "PR" out (Phrenic, Recurrent laryngeal). Yawning chemistry: "DOA" — Dopamine → Oxytocin → Acetylcholine (stop sign = Opioids).
How to write this in the exam Draw the reflex arc first — it shows instantly that you understand the mechanism. Then list causes in a 2-column table (benign vs persistent). For yawning, give the 3 phases, the PVN/neurotransmitter story, then theories (state that the O2/CO2 theory is not supported — this impresses). Finish with the comparison table.
Mistakes that cost marks (1) Saying the glottis closes before the diaphragm contracts — it closes ~35 ms after. (2) Forgetting the afferent sympathetic fibres T6–T12. (3) Stating "we yawn because of low oxygen" as fact. (4) Treating hiccups purely as a nuisance — always mention persistent/intractable hiccups as a sign of disease.
