Apoptosis is a tightly regulated and highly efficient cell death program which requires the interplay of multitude of factors. Discuss.

What the examiner wants The key words in the question are "tightly regulated", "efficient" and "multitude of factors". So: define apoptosis; show the intrinsic (mitochondrial) and extrinsic (death-receptor) pathways converging on caspases; name the regulators (Bcl-2 family, p53, IAPs/Smac, FLIP, cytochrome c, Apaf-1); describe the execution phase and silent phagocytosis (why it is "efficient" — no inflammation); give physiological and pathological examples and diseases of too much/too little apoptosis; compare with necrosis.

Definition

Apoptosis (Greek: "falling off", as leaves from a tree) is programmed cell death — an energy (ATP)-dependent, genetically controlled pathway in which a cell activates enzymes (caspases) that degrade its own nuclear DNA and proteins, and is then removed by phagocytes without provoking inflammation. It is the body's way of eliminating unwanted, aged or damaged cells — the "cellular suicide" counterpart of mitosis.

Why "tightly regulated" and "efficient"

  1. Every step is governed by a balance of pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic molecules; the cell dies only when the balance tips.
  2. The killing enzymes (caspases) exist as inactive zymogens (procaspases) and are activated by a cascade — amplification makes it rapid and irreversible once committed.
  3. The cell membrane stays intact; the cell is cut into membrane-bound apoptotic bodies that display "eat-me" signals and are swallowed within minutes — contents never leak, so no inflammation and no damage to neighbours.

Morphological features

  1. Cell shrinkage with dense cytoplasm and tightly packed organelles.
  2. Chromatin condensation (pyknosis) at the nuclear membrane → nuclear fragmentation (karyorrhexis).
  3. Membrane blebbing → formation of apoptotic bodies.
  4. Phagocytosis by macrophages/neighbouring cells; no inflammatory reaction.
  5. Biochemically: internucleosomal DNA cleavage into 180–200 bp fragments → "DNA ladder" on gel electrophoresis.

The factors involved (the "multitude of factors")

FactorRole
Caspases (cysteine-aspartic proteases)Initiators: caspase-8, -10 (extrinsic), caspase-9 (intrinsic). Executioners: caspase-3, -6, -7. Inflammatory caspase-1 is NOT apoptotic (pyroptosis).

p53 ("guardian of the genome")Detects DNA damage → arrests cell cycle for repair; if irreparable, ↑ Puma, Noxa, Bax → apoptosis.

Mechanism — phases and pathways

A. Initiation phase
  1. Intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway — the major pathway. Stimuli: DNA damage (radiation, chemotherapy, free radicals), growth-factor/hormone withdrawal, hypoxia, misfolded proteins (ER stress), ↑ cytosolic Ca2+.
    • Stress activates BH3-only sensors (and p53) → they inhibit Bcl-2/Bcl-xL and activate Bax and Bak.
    • Bax/Bak oligomerise and form pores in the outer mitochondrial membrane — mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilisation (MOMP).
    • Cytochrome c and Smac/DIABLO leak into the cytosol. Cytochrome c + Apaf-1 + dATP + procaspase-9 = apoptosome → active caspase-9.
  2. Extrinsic (death-receptor) pathway — e.g. elimination of self-reactive lymphocytes, CTL killing.
    • FasL (on activated T cells) binds trimerised Fas; the cytoplasmic "death domains" recruit FADD → DISC.
    • DISC brings procaspase-8 molecules together → auto-activation → active caspase-8.
    • Cross-talk: caspase-8 cleaves Bid → tBid, which activates Bax/Bak → amplifies via the mitochondrial pathway.
B. Execution phase
  1. Caspase-8 and -9 cleave and activate executioner caspases-3, -6, -7.
  2. Caspase-3 cleaves ICAD, freeing CAD (caspase-activated DNase) → internucleosomal DNA cleavage.
  3. Cleavage of nuclear lamins (nuclear collapse), cytoskeletal proteins (shrinkage, blebbing), DNA-repair enzymes (PARP).
C. Removal of dead cells

Phosphatidylserine flips from the inner to the outer leaflet of the membrane ("eat-me" signal); thrombospondin and C1q also bind; macrophages engulf the apoptotic bodies rapidly and release anti-inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β, IL-10).

Significance — physiological and pathological apoptosis

PhysiologicalPathological (useful)
1. Embryogenesis — removal of interdigital webs, formation of lumens, sculpting of the nervous system.
2. Hormone-dependent involution — endometrium at menstruation, ovarian follicular atresia, post-lactation breast, prostate after castration.
3. Cell turnover in proliferating epithelia (intestinal crypts, skin).
4. Deletion of self-reactive lymphocytes in the thymus (immune tolerance).
5. Death of neutrophils and lymphocytes at the end of an immune response.		 1. Cells with irreparable DNA damage (radiation, cytotoxic drugs, hypoxia).
2. Accumulation of misfolded proteins (ER stress).
3. Virus-infected cells, e.g. viral hepatitis (Councilman bodies), killed by CTLs.
4. Atrophy after duct obstruction (pancreas, parotid, kidney).
5. Tumour cell death in response to therapy.
Diseases of dysregulated apoptosis
  1. Too little apoptosis → cell survival: cancer — e.g. follicular lymphoma (t(14;18) → Bcl-2 over-expression), p53 mutation in many cancers; autoimmune disease — defective Fas/FasL (autoimmune lymphoproliferative syndrome) lets self-reactive lymphocytes survive; persistent viral infection.
  2. Too much apoptosis → cell loss: neurodegenerative disease (Alzheimer, Parkinson, ALS), ischaemic injury (MI penumbra, stroke), loss of CD4 T cells in AIDS, heart failure, toxin-induced liver injury.

Conclusion

Apoptosis is a genetically programmed, caspase-driven suicide whose initiation (intrinsic or extrinsic), amplification and execution are each balanced by pro- and anti-apoptotic factors; its clean phagocytic removal makes it efficient and non-inflammatory. Too little apoptosis favours cancer and autoimmunity; too much causes degenerative and ischaemic disease.

Understand it (plain English) Think of apoptosis as a controlled demolition rather than a building collapse (necrosis). The order to demolish comes either from outside (a death ligand "knocks" on the Fas door — extrinsic) or from inside (the mitochondria are told the cell is too damaged — intrinsic). Both orders go to the same demolition crew: caspase-3. Bcl-2 is the security guard on the mitochondria; Bax/Bak are the people drilling holes. The building is taken down floor by floor, wrapped in plastic (apoptotic bodies) and carted away by macrophages — no dust, no mess (no inflammation).
Memory trick Initiator caspases: "8 Outside, 9 Inside" (extrinsic 8, intrinsic 9). Executioners: "3, 6, 7 send the cell to heaven." Bcl-2 family: "Bcl-2 Blocks, Bax Breaks". Morphology: "Shrink, Condense, Bleb, Eat".

Mistakes that cost marks (1) Saying apoptosis causes inflammation. (2) Putting caspase-8 in the intrinsic pathway or caspase-9 in the extrinsic. (3) Calling Bcl-2 pro-apoptotic. (4) Writing only "programmed cell death" without describing the pathways — that is a 2-mark answer, not a distinction.
