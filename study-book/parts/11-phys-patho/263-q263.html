Introduction — the normal conductive system

The heart beats rhythmically because impulses arise in the sinoatrial (SA) node — the pacemaker — and spread in a fixed order: SA node → atrial muscle and internodal fibres (anterior, middle, posterior; Bachmann's bundle to left atrium) → atrioventricular (AV) node (where conduction is delayed ~0.1 s so the atria empty first) → bundle of His → right and left bundle branches → Purkinje fibres → ventricular muscle. Rates of impulse generation: SA node 70–80/min, AV node 40–60/min, Purkinje/ventricular tissue 20–40/min. The fastest tissue sets the rhythm.

PART A — CARDIAC ARRHYTHMIA

Definition

Cardiac arrhythmia (dysrhythmia) is any abnormality in the rate, rhythm, site of origin or conduction of the cardiac impulse.

Mechanisms (pathophysiology)
  1. Disorders of impulse formation
    • Altered normal automaticity: SA node fires faster (sympathetic, fever, thyrotoxicosis → sinus tachycardia) or slower (vagal tone, athletes, hypothyroidism → sinus bradycardia).
    • Abnormal automaticity / ectopic focus: ischaemia, stretch, hypokalaemia, catecholamines or digitalis make atrial, junctional or ventricular cells partially depolarised so they fire spontaneously → ectopic beats and tachycardias.
    • Triggered activity: after-depolarisations — early (during phase 2–3; prolonged QT, hypokalaemia → torsades de pointes) and delayed (after repolarisation; Ca2+ overload, digitalis toxicity).
  2. Disorders of impulse conduction
    • Re-entry (circus movement): requires (i) two pathways, (ii) unidirectional block in one, and (iii) slow conduction so that the impulse returns to tissue no longer refractory → the impulse circles repeatedly. Basis of paroxysmal SVT, atrial flutter, WPW tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia and fibrillation.
    • Conduction block: delay or failure of conduction → heart block (Part B).

Consequences

Very fast rates shorten diastole → ↓ ventricular filling and ↓ coronary perfusion → ↓ cardiac output, angina, hypotension; very slow rates → ↓ cardiac output → syncope; loss of atrial "kick" in AF reduces filling by ~20–30%; stasis in fibrillating atria → thrombo-embolism.

PART B — HEART BLOCK

Definition

Heart block is a delay or complete failure of conduction of the impulse at some point in the conductive system, so the impulse is slowed or not transmitted from the pacemaker to the ventricles.

Causes

Ischaemic heart disease / inferior MI (AV nodal artery), degenerative fibrosis of the conducting system (elderly), myocarditis (rheumatic fever, diphtheria), drugs (digitalis, β-blockers, verapamil), ↑ vagal tone, hyperkalaemia, congenital (maternal anti-Ro antibodies), cardiac surgery.

Types of heart block
  1. Sinoatrial (SA) block: the impulse from the SA node fails to reach the atria → whole beats (P + QRS + T) are missing. The AV node may take over as pacemaker (nodal rhythm).
  2. Atrioventricular (AV) block — the commonest; conduction from atria to ventricles is affected:
    • First-degree (incomplete): every impulse reaches the ventricles but slowly — PR interval > 0.20 s, constant. No missed beats; asymptomatic.
    • Second-degree (partial): some impulses fail to reach the ventricles → dropped beats (P without QRS).
      Mobitz type I (Wenckebach phenomenon): PR interval progressively lengthens until one P wave is not followed by a QRS; then the cycle repeats. Block usually in the AV node; often benign (vagal, inferior MI).
      Mobitz type II: PR constant, but suddenly a QRS is dropped; may occur in fixed ratios (2:1, 3:1). Block below the AV node (His–Purkinje); may progress to complete block.
    • Third-degree (complete) AV block: no impulses pass. Atria beat at SA rate (70–80/min) and ventricles at their own idioventricular rhythm (20–40/min) — AV dissociation: P waves and QRS complexes are completely independent; QRS may be wide.
  3. Bundle branch block (BBB): one bundle branch fails; the impulse reaches that ventricle late via muscle-to-muscle spread → the two ventricles contract asynchronously → wide QRS (> 0.12 s), splitting of heart sounds. Right BBB or left BBB (left is more often pathological).
Stokes–Adams syndrome

When an incomplete AV block suddenly becomes complete, the ventricles stop beating for some seconds before the idioventricular pacemaker "escapes" (pre-automatic pause). Cerebral blood flow falls → sudden fainting (syncope), pallor, and if prolonged, convulsions — Stokes–Adams attack. Recovery is accompanied by flushing as circulation resumes. Very slow idioventricular rates also cause fatigue and heart failure. (Management outline: artificial pacemaker.)

Conclusion

Arrhythmias arise from disorders of impulse formation (automaticity, triggered activity) or conduction (re-entry, block). Heart block is a conduction failure at the SA node, AV node or bundle branches, graded 1°–3° at the AV level and recognised by PR changes and dropped or dissociated QRS complexes.

Understand it (plain English) Picture the conduction system as a relay race. Arrhythmia = either the starter misbehaves (SA node too fast/slow, or a "rogue runner" — an ectopic focus — starts his own race) or the baton passing goes wrong (re-entry: the baton loops round and round). Heart block is a baton-passing problem at a specific handover: 1° = slow handover, every baton arrives late; 2° = some batons dropped (Wenckebach: the runner gets slower and slower until he drops one, then rests and recovers); 3° = the handover is completely broken, so the ventricle team starts its own slow race.
Memory trick "If R is far from P, you've got a FIRST degree.
Longer, longer, longer, drop — then you have a WENCKEBACH.
If some Ps don't get through, then you have a MOBITZ II.
If Ps and Qs don't agree, then you have a THIRD degree." Re-entry needs "2-1-S": 2 pathways, 1-way block, Slow conduction.

Mistakes that cost marks (1) Calling first-degree block a "dropped beat" — no beats are dropped. (2) Swapping Mobitz I and II (I = progressive PR prolongation). (3) Forgetting AV dissociation and the idioventricular rate in complete block. (4) Not explaining why AF causes stroke (atrial stasis → thrombus).
