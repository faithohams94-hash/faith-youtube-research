Definition

Defecation is the voiding of faeces from the rectum through the anus. It is a spinal reflex (centre in the sacral segments of the spinal cord) that, after infancy, is placed under voluntary control by higher centres.

Filling of the rectum

  1. The rectum is normally empty, because a weak functional sphincter at the rectosigmoid junction and the sharp angle there hold faeces back.
  2. Mass movements of the colon, often started by the gastrocolic and duodenocolic reflexes after a meal, push faeces into the rectum.
  3. Distension of the rectal wall stimulates stretch receptors. When rectal pressure rises to about 18 mmHg the desire to defecate is felt. At about 55 mmHg both sphincters relax and defecation occurs reflexly, even without voluntary consent.

Mechanism: the defecation reflexes

1. Intrinsic (myenteric) defecation reflex

Rectal distension→Myenteric plexus→Peristaltic waves in descending colon, sigmoid, rectum→Internal sphincter relaxes (receptive relaxation)

On its own this reflex is weak. It needs to be reinforced by the spinal reflex.

2. Parasympathetic (spinal) defecation reflex: the main reflex

  1. Receptors: stretch receptors in the rectal wall.
  2. Afferent: sensory fibres in the pelvic nerves to the sacral cord.
  3. Centre: defecation centre in the sacral segments S2–S4 (S2, S3, S4) of the spinal cord.
  4. Efferent: parasympathetic fibres in the pelvic nerves (nervi erigentes).
  5. Effects: strong contraction of the descending colon, sigmoid colon and rectum (the intrinsic waves are intensified many times), and relaxation of the internal anal sphincter.
  6. If the external sphincter is also relaxed voluntarily (pudendal inhibition), faeces are expelled.
  7. Signals entering the cord also cause a deep breath, closure of the glottis and contraction of the abdominal muscles, which push faeces downward, and the pelvic floor is drawn up over the faeces.

Facilitatory mechanisms (help defecation)

  1. Valsalva manoeuvre / straining: deep inspiration, closure of the glottis and forceful contraction of the abdominal muscles and diaphragm raise intra-abdominal pressure, which squeezes the colon and rectum.
  2. Voluntary relaxation of the external anal sphincter by the cortex inhibiting the pudendal motor neurons.
  3. Contraction of the levator ani (pelvic floor), which pulls the anal canal up over the faecal mass, then relaxation of the puborectalis, which straightens the anorectal angle.
  4. Squatting posture, which straightens the anorectal angle and helps raise abdominal pressure.
  5. Gastrocolic and duodenocolic reflexes: a meal starts mass movements and fills the rectum, so the urge is common after breakfast.
  6. Parasympathetic (pelvic) stimulation and higher-centre facilitation once the time is convenient.

Inhibitory mechanisms (delay or prevent defecation)

  1. Voluntary contraction of the external anal sphincter (cortex → pudendal nerve) when the time or place is not convenient. The rectal wall then relaxes (adapts), the pressure falls, and the reflex dies out for some hours until the next mass movement.
  2. Higher centres: cerebral cortex (conscious control learned during toilet training), and emotional factors acting through the hypothalamus.
  3. Sympathetic (hypogastric) stimulation from L1–L2, which inhibits colonic movements and contracts the internal anal sphincter.
  4. Contraction of the puborectalis, which keeps the anorectal angle sharp.
  5. Repeated suppression of the urge makes the reflex less sensitive and leads to constipation.

Applied physiology

  • Infants: defecation is purely reflex, because the voluntary pathways are not yet myelinated.
  • Spinal cord transection above the sacral segments: the reflex arc is intact but voluntary control is lost. The result is automatic (reflex) defecation, and the patient can trigger it by stimulating the anal canal.
  • Destruction of the sacral segments or cauda equina: the parasympathetic reflex is lost. Only the weak intrinsic reflex remains, giving constipation with faecal incontinence.
  • Hirschsprung's disease: absent myenteric plexus in a segment (congenital megacolon), so the intrinsic reflex fails.

Conclusion

Defecation is a sacral (S2–S4) parasympathetic reflex, helped by a weak myenteric reflex. The cortex can facilitate it (Valsalva, relaxing the external sphincter) or inhibit it (contracting the external sphincter through the pudendal nerve), so it is a voluntary act built on an involuntary reflex.

Understand it (plain English) Kris, there are two gates. The inner gate (internal sphincter) is automatic: when the rectum stretches, the sacral cord tells the colon to squeeze and the inner gate to open. The outer gate (external sphincter) belongs to you, through the pudendal nerve. If you keep it shut, the rectum slowly relaxes and the urge fades. That is the inhibition. When you decide to go, you open the outer gate and strain (Valsalva). That is the facilitation.
Memory trick "S2, 3, 4 keeps the poo off the floor": pelvic splanchnic and pudendal nerves both come from S2–S4. Facilitators are "VALSALVA PUSH": Valsalva, Posture (squat), Unlock external sphincter, Stimulus from food (gastrocolic), Higher centres permit.

Mistakes that cost marks Saying the internal sphincter is voluntary. Giving the vagus as the efferent nerve (it is the pelvic nerves, S2–S4). Forgetting the pudendal nerve. Leaving out the "rectum is normally empty and mass movement fills it" step.
