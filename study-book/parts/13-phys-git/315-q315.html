Introduction

The GIT has a double nerve supply. An intrinsic nerve supply, the enteric nervous system, lies inside the gut wall and can work on its own. An extrinsic nerve supply from the autonomic nervous system modifies it. Somatic nerves supply only the skeletal muscle at the two ends (mouth, pharynx and upper oesophagus; external anal sphincter).

A. Intrinsic nerve supply: the enteric nervous system (ENS)

The ENS runs from the oesophagus to the anus and contains about 100 million neurons, roughly as many as the spinal cord. It is sometimes called the "brain of the gut". It has two plexuses:

Neurons and neurotransmitters of the ENS

  • Excitatory: acetylcholine (main one), substance P.
  • Inhibitory: vasoactive intestinal polypeptide (VIP), nitric oxide (NO), ATP. These relax sphincters and the muscle ahead of a bolus.
  • Others: serotonin (5-HT), somatostatin, enkephalins, GRP (gastrin-releasing peptide, which releases gastrin).
  • The ENS contains sensory neurons (stretch and chemoreceptors in the epithelium and wall), interneurons and motor neurons, so it can complete local reflexes, such as the peristaltic reflex (law of the gut), without the CNS.

B. Extrinsic nerve supply (autonomic)

1. Parasympathetic: mainly excitatory

  • Cranial outflow (vagus, X): supplies the oesophagus, stomach, pancreas, gallbladder, small intestine and the colon up to about the proximal part of the transverse colon. Mouth and pharynx: VII and IX (salivary glands).
  • Sacral outflow (pelvic nerves, S2–S4): supplies the distal colon, sigmoid, rectum and anal canal. These fibres are important in the defecation reflex.
  • Preganglionic fibres synapse on neurons of the enteric plexuses. Postganglionic fibres are cholinergic.
  • Effects: ↑ motility and tone, ↑ secretion of glands, relaxation of most sphincters, ↑ blood flow.

2. Sympathetic: mainly inhibitory

  • Preganglionic fibres arise from lateral horn cells of T5 to L2. They pass through the splanchnic nerves and synapse in the coeliac, superior mesenteric and inferior mesenteric (prevertebral) ganglia.
  • Postganglionic fibres are noradrenergic and supply all parts of the gut.
  • Effects: ↓ motility (inhibition of ENS neurons), contraction of sphincters (internal anal, ileocaecal, pyloric), vasoconstriction, slight ↓ secretion. Strong stimulation can block the movement of food (functional ileus).

3. Afferent (sensory) fibres

  • Arise from receptors in the mucosa and wall that respond to irritation, distension and chemicals.
  • About 80% of vagal fibres are afferent. They carry information to the medulla for vagovagal reflexes.
  • Others travel with sympathetic nerves to the spinal cord and prevertebral ganglia. They carry visceral pain, which is referred to dermatomes.

C. Gastrointestinal reflexes (how the system is used)

  1. Local reflexes within the ENS: peristalsis, local secretion and mixing.
  2. Gut → prevertebral sympathetic ganglia → gut: gastrocolic reflex (stomach starts colonic evacuation), enterogastric reflex (intestine inhibits gastric motility and secretion), colonoileal reflex (colon inhibits emptying of the ileum).
  3. Gut → spinal cord or brainstem → gut: vagovagal reflexes controlling gastric motility and secretion, pain reflexes that inhibit the whole gut, and the defecation reflex (sacral cord).

Applied physiology

  • Hirschsprung's disease (congenital megacolon): congenital absence of the myenteric and submucous plexuses in a segment of colon. The segment cannot relax, so faeces accumulate above it and the proximal colon dilates.
  • Achalasia cardia: degeneration of the myenteric plexus of the lower oesophagus means the LES fails to relax.
  • Vagotomy, once used for peptic ulcer, reduces gastric acid secretion but also slows gastric emptying.
  • Paralytic ileus: after abdominal surgery or peritonitis, strong sympathetic inhibition stops intestinal movement.

Conclusion

The GIT is controlled mainly by its own enteric nervous system: the myenteric plexus for motility and the submucous plexus for secretion and blood flow. Parasympathetic nerves excite it and sympathetic nerves inhibit it, and afferent fibres close the reflex loops.

Understand it (plain English) The gut has its own local "brain" (the ENS) that can run digestion even if the vagus is cut. The outer plexus sits between the two muscle coats, so it controls movement. The inner plexus sits near the mucosa, so it controls secretion and absorption. The autonomic nerves act like a volume knob: parasympathetic turns digestion up (rest and digest), sympathetic turns it down (fight or flight), shutting the gates (sphincters) and diverting blood away.
