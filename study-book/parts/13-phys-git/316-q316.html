What the examiner wants Salivary glands and their contribution, volume and properties of saliva, the two-stage mechanism of secretion (acini make a primary isotonic fluid, ducts modify it into hypotonic saliva) and how flow rate changes composition. Then regulation, which is purely nervous: parasympathetic (superior and inferior salivatory nuclei, VII, IX, ganglia) and sympathetic (T1–T2, superior cervical ganglion), with their effects; unconditioned and conditioned reflexes; and the factors and disorders that change flow (xerostomia, ptyalism).

Introduction

Saliva is the secretion of the salivary glands into the mouth. About 1000–1500 mL is secreted per day. It is hypotonic, with a pH of about 6.0–7.0, and is 99.5% water and 0.5% solids (ptyalin or salivary amylase, lingual lipase, mucin, lysozyme, IgA, electrolytes).

Many small buccal glands also secrete mucus. At rest, saliva flows at about 0.5 mL/min. Flow falls almost to nil during sleep and can rise several-fold during eating.

Salivary flow: mechanism of secretion (two stages)

  1. Primary secretion by the acini: the acinar cells secrete a fluid containing ptyalin and/or mucin in a solution of ions whose concentrations are similar to ECF (isotonic). Acetylcholine (M3 receptors) raises intracellular Ca2+, which opens Cl− channels. Na+ and water follow.
  2. Modification in the ducts: as the fluid flows through the intercalated and striated ducts:
    • Na+ is actively reabsorbed and Cl− is reabsorbed passively with it.
    • K+ and HCO3− are secreted into the lumen.
    • The ducts are relatively impermeable to water, so the final saliva becomes hypotonic. It has low Na+ and Cl− and high K+ and HCO3− compared with plasma.
  3. Effect of flow rate: when flow is fast, the fluid spends less time in the ducts and is modified less. Na+ and Cl− rise towards plasma values and HCO3− rises (it is also actively stimulated). K+ falls slightly, and saliva becomes less hypotonic but stays hypotonic.
  4. Aldosterone increases Na+ reabsorption and K+ secretion in the ducts, as it does in the renal tubule.

Regulation of salivary secretion

Salivary secretion is regulated only by nervous mechanisms; no hormone directly starts it. Both divisions of the autonomic nervous system increase secretion, but the type of saliva differs.

1. Parasympathetic supply (main control)

  • Submandibular and sublingual glands: superior salivatory nucleus (pons) → fibres in the facial nerve (VII) → chorda tympani → joins the lingual nerve → submandibular ganglion → postganglionic fibres to the glands.
  • Parotid gland: inferior salivatory nucleus (medulla) → glossopharyngeal nerve (IX) → tympanic branch → lesser petrosal nerve → otic ganglion → postganglionic fibres through the auriculotemporal nerve → parotid.
  • Effect: acetylcholine is released, giving a large volume of watery saliva that is low in organic matter. It also causes vasodilatation of the glands (through VIP and kallikrein, which produces bradykinin).
  • Atropine blocks this, so it causes a dry mouth.

2. Sympathetic supply

  • Preganglionic fibres arise from the lateral horns of T1–T2 → superior cervical ganglion → postganglionic fibres travel along the plexus around the external carotid artery to all the glands.
  • Effect: noradrenaline is released, giving a small quantity of thick, viscous saliva rich in organic substances (mucus). It also causes vasoconstriction.

3. Reflex regulation (Pavlov)

  1. Unconditioned reflex: inborn, no learning needed. Food in the mouth stimulates taste buds and touch receptors. Impulses travel through V, VII, IX and X to the nucleus of the tractus solitarius and then the salivatory nuclei, which send efferent impulses to the glands. Smooth objects cause little secretion; rough or sour material causes more.
  2. Conditioned reflex: acquired by experience. The sight, smell, hearing (Pavlov's bell) or thought of food stimulates the cortex and hypothalamus (appetite area), which then stimulate the salivatory nuclei. It needs previous experience and an intact cortex.
  3. Saliva is also secreted reflexly from the stomach and upper intestine, for example when irritating food is swallowed or before vomiting (nausea).

Factors affecting salivary flow

  • Increase: food (especially sour or acidic), chewing, parasympathomimetic drugs (pilocarpine), nausea, conditioned stimuli.
  • Decrease: sleep, dehydration, fear and anxiety, atropine and anticholinergic drugs, fever.

Applied physiology

  • Hyposalivation and xerostomia (dry mouth): from dehydration, fever, atropine, Sjögren's syndrome or radiotherapy. Leads to dental caries, mouth infection and difficulty in swallowing and speaking.
  • Hypersalivation (ptyalism, sialorrhoea): in teething, pregnancy, oral ulcers, nausea, Parkinsonism and mercury poisoning.
  • Mumps: viral infection of the parotid, causing painful swelling.

Conclusion

Saliva is formed in two stages, so its composition depends on flow rate. Its secretion is purely nervous: mainly parasympathetic through VII and IX, triggered by unconditioned and conditioned reflexes, with the sympathetic adding a scanty, viscous, mucus-rich saliva.

Understand it (plain English) The acinus makes "plasma-like water". The duct then pulls out the salt (Na⁺, Cl⁻) but cannot pull out water, so what reaches your mouth is dilute (hypotonic). If you salivate fast, the duct has no time to pull the salt out, so fast saliva is saltier. For the nerves: "VII goes to the two glands under the tongue, IX goes to the parotid." Parasympathetic gives a flood of water; sympathetic gives a little thick mucus, which is why your mouth feels dry and sticky when you are scared.
Memory trick "7 Sisters Sub, 9 Out to Parotid": VII → Submandibular ganglion → Sub-glands; IX → Otic ganglion → Parotid. Shares: "25-70-5: Parotid, SubMandibular, SubLingual".
