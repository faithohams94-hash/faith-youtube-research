Introduction

Gregor Johann Mendel (1822–1884), an Austrian monk, is the "Father of Genetics". Working with the garden pea (Pisum sativum) he studied 7 pairs of contrasting characters (e.g. tall/dwarf stem, round/wrinkled seed, yellow/green seed). He published in 1866; his work was rediscovered in 1900 by de Vries, Correns and Tschermak. He called the hereditary units "factors" — today called genes, and their alternative forms alleles.

Why pea was ideal: self-pollinating (pure lines easily obtained), easy to cross-pollinate, short generation time, many offspring (statistics possible), clear-cut contrasting characters.

Key terms used

  • Genotype — the genetic constitution (TT, Tt, tt). Phenotype — the observable character (tall, dwarf).
  • Homozygous — two identical alleles (TT, tt). Heterozygous — two different alleles (Tt).
  • Dominant allele (capital letter) expresses itself in the heterozygote; recessive (small letter) is expressed only when homozygous.
  • P = parental, F1 = first filial, F2 = second filial generation. Monohybrid cross = one pair of characters; dihybrid cross = two pairs.

1. Law of Dominance

Statement: When two homozygous individuals with one or more contrasting characters are crossed, the character that appears in the F1 is the dominant one and the one that is masked (not expressed) is the recessive one.

Illustration: Pure tall (TT) × pure dwarf (tt) → F1 all tall (Tt). Dwarfness is not lost — it is only hidden, and reappears in F2.

2. Law of Segregation (Law of Purity of Gametes) — Mendel's First Law

Statement: The two alleles of a gene pair, which remain together in the individual, separate (segregate) during gamete formation, so that each gamete receives only ONE allele of the pair. Gametes are therefore always "pure".

Illustration (monohybrid cross): F1 Tt × Tt → gametes T and t in equal numbers from each parent → F2 genotypes 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt; phenotypes 3 tall : 1 dwarf.

Cellular basis: separation of homologous chromosomes at anaphase I of meiosis.

3. Law of Independent Assortment — Mendel's Second Law

Statement: When two or more pairs of contrasting characters are considered together, the alleles of each pair segregate independently of the alleles of the other pair during gamete formation, so that all combinations of alleles appear in the gametes with equal frequency.

Illustration (dihybrid cross): Round yellow (RRYY) × wrinkled green (rryy) → F1 all round yellow (RrYy). F1 × F1: each F1 forms 4 kinds of gametes — RY, Ry, rY, ry (¼ each) → 16 combinations → F2 phenotypic ratio 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. Two new combinations (round green, wrinkled yellow) appear, proving the genes assorted independently. Each character alone still gives 12 : 4 = 3 : 1.

Cellular basis: random orientation of different homologous pairs on the metaphase I plate. It holds for genes on different chromosomes (or far apart on the same chromosome).

Exceptions (non-Mendelian patterns)

  1. Incomplete dominance — heterozygote is intermediate (e.g. 4 o'clock plant Mirabilis: red × white → pink; F2 1 : 2 : 1).
  2. Codominance — both alleles expressed: blood group AB (IAIB); HbAS (sickle cell trait).
  3. Linkage — genes close together on the same chromosome do not assort independently.
  4. Multiple alleles (ABO: IA, IB, i), polygenic inheritance (height, skin colour), epistasis, mitochondrial (maternal) inheritance.

Medical application

Mendelian laws explain single-gene inheritance in man. Example: two sickle cell carriers (HbAS × HbAS) → 1 HbAA : 2 HbAS : 1 HbSS, i.e. a 25% risk of sickle cell anaemia in each pregnancy — the basis of genetic counselling and genotype testing before marriage.

Conclusion

Mendel's three laws — dominance, segregation and independent assortment — are explained by the behaviour of chromosomes in meiosis and remain the foundation for predicting inheritance of single-gene disorders.

Understand it (plain English) Every person (or pea) carries two copies of each gene, one from each parent. Dominance: if one copy "shouts" (T) it drowns out the "whisper" (t). Segregation: when making sperm or eggs you can only pack one copy into each — like dealing one card from a pair. Independent assortment: the card you deal from the "height" pair has no influence on the card you deal from the "colour" pair — because they sit on different chromosomes that line up randomly.
Memory trick "D-S-I" = Don't Stop Inheriting → Dominance, Segregation, Independent assortment. Ratios: "3-1 for one, 9-3-3-1 for two".
