Introduction

A genetic disorder is a disease caused wholly or partly by an abnormality of the genetic material — a mutation in a single gene, an abnormality of chromosome number or structure, or the combined effect of many genes and the environment. Traditionally they are grouped into three main classes:

  1. Single-gene (Mendelian / monogenic) disorders
  2. Chromosomal disorders
  3. Multifactorial (polygenic) disorders

1. Single-gene (Mendelian) disorders

Definition: caused by a mutation in one gene (one or both alleles); they follow Mendel's laws and show characteristic pedigree patterns. Individually rare, but over 5,000 are known.

2. Chromosomal disorders

Definition: caused by an abnormality in the number or structure of chromosomes, visible on karyotype. They involve many genes at once, so usually produce multiple malformations and mental retardation; they are a major cause of spontaneous abortion (~50% of first-trimester abortuses).

(a) Numerical abnormalities
  • Aneuploidy — gain or loss of one chromosome, mainly due to non-disjunction in meiosis (risk rises with maternal age).
    • Trisomy (2n+1 = 47): Down syndrome (+21), Edwards (+18), Patau (+13), Klinefelter (47,XXY).
    • Monosomy (2n−1 = 45): Turner syndrome (45,X) — the only viable full monosomy.
  • Polyploidy — whole extra sets: triploidy (69), tetraploidy (92) — usually lethal (abortion, hydatidiform mole).
(b) Structural abnormalities

Result from chromosome breakage and abnormal reunion: deletion (cri-du-chat, 5p−), duplication, translocation — reciprocal or Robertsonian (14;21 familial Down; 9;22 Philadelphia chromosome), inversion (pericentric, paracentric), ring chromosome, isochromosome (i(Xq) in Turner).

3. Multifactorial (polygenic) disorders

Definition: caused by the interaction of many genes (each with a small additive effect) with environmental factors. They do not follow Mendelian ratios.

  1. Threshold model — liability is normally distributed in the population; disease appears only when liability crosses a threshold.
  2. Familial clustering without a clear pattern; recurrence risk in first-degree relatives is low (about 2–5%) but higher than in the general population.
  3. Risk is higher when more relatives are affected, when the proband is severely affected, and when the proband is of the less commonly affected sex.
  4. They are the commonest genetic disorders.

Examples: congenital malformations — cleft lip/palate, neural tube defects (anencephaly, spina bifida), congenital heart disease, congenital dislocation of hip, club foot, congenital hypertrophic pyloric stenosis; adult diseases — diabetes mellitus, essential hypertension, coronary artery disease, schizophrenia, asthma.

Other classes (mention for completeness)

  • Mitochondrial disorders — mutation in mitochondrial DNA; maternal inheritance (all children of an affected mother, none from father), e.g. Leber hereditary optic neuropathy.
  • Somatic cell genetic disorders — mutations acquired in body cells, e.g. most cancers.

Conclusion

Genetic disorders are mainly single-gene, chromosomal or multifactorial. Knowing the class tells us the mechanism, the recurrence risk and the test to request — the basis of diagnosis, prevention and genetic counselling.

Understand it (plain English) Think of the genome as a library. A single-gene disorder is one misspelt word in one book (sickle cell: one letter changed in the β-globin gene). A chromosomal disorder is a whole extra volume, a missing volume or torn pages (Down = an extra copy of book 21). A multifactorial disorder is many slightly odd books plus a bad reading environment — none alone causes disease, but together they tip you over a threshold.
Memory trick "S-C-M" — Single, Chromosomal, Multifactorial ("Some Cases Multiply"). X-linked recessive list: "Hemo-Duchenne-G6PD-Colour-Lesch" — "He Did Get Colour Lessons".
How to write this in the exam Intro listing the 3 classes → one heading per class with definition, types, features, examples → pedigrees and non-disjunction diagram → comparison table → conclusion. Quote Nigerian-relevant examples (sickle cell, G6PD deficiency) — examiners like local relevance.
Mistakes that cost marks Calling Down syndrome "inherited" in the Mendelian sense (most cases are sporadic non-disjunction). Listing Klinefelter as monosomy (it is a trisomy, 47,XXY). Saying haemophilia occurs equally in both sexes. Forgetting the environmental component of multifactorial disorders.
