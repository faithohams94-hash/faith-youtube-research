Discuss the importance of Genetics to Medical practice.

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants Definition of genetics/medical genetics; the burden of genetic disease; then 8–10 well-headed areas of importance (diagnosis, counselling, prenatal diagnosis, screening, pharmacogenetics, therapy, cancer, forensic/paternity, transfusion/transplant, understanding common diseases, prevention), each with a concrete example — sickle cell scores well locally.

Introduction

Genetics is the science of heredity and variation. Medical (human) genetics applies it to the diagnosis, management and prevention of disease in man. Genetic factors contribute to almost every disease — from single-gene disorders like sickle cell anaemia to multifactorial diseases like diabetes and hypertension. About 2–3% of newborns have a major congenital anomaly, many genetic, and chromosomal abnormalities account for about half of first-trimester miscarriages. Hence every doctor needs genetics.

Importance of genetics to medical practice

  1. Diagnosis of genetic disease — recognising Mendelian patterns from a pedigree; chromosome analysis (karyotype, FISH) for Down, Turner, Klinefelter syndromes; haemoglobin electrophoresis and DNA tests for sickle cell disease and thalassaemia; enzyme assays for inborn errors of metabolism (phenylketonuria, galactosaemia).
  2. Genetic counselling — explaining the nature of a disorder, its recurrence risk (50% AD, 25% AR, 50% of sons for XR carriers) and options to families. In Nigeria, pre-marital genotype (HbAS) testing is a direct application.
  3. Prenatal diagnosis — amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling, fetal ultrasound and maternal serum markers (α-fetoprotein raised in neural tube defects) allow detection of chromosomal disorders, sickle cell disease and neural tube defects before birth.
  4. Screening — carrier screening (sickle cell, thalassaemia), newborn screening (phenylketonuria, congenital hypothyroidism, sickle cell) for early treatment to prevent damage.
  5. Pharmacogenetics — inherited differences in drug response: G6PD deficiency → haemolysis with primaquine, sulphonamides, fava beans; slow acetylators → isoniazid neuropathy; pseudocholinesterase deficiency → prolonged apnoea after succinylcholine; malignant hyperthermia with halothane.
  6. Treatment — dietary therapy (low-phenylalanine diet in PKU, galactose-free diet), replacement of the missing product (factor VIII in haemophilia, insulin), recombinant DNA products (human insulin, growth hormone, vaccines), and gene therapy (e.g. ADA-deficient SCID).
  7. Cancer genetics — cancers result from mutations in oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes (p53, RB). Inherited cancer syndromes (retinoblastoma, BRCA1/2 breast–ovarian cancer, familial polyposis coli) allow screening of relatives; Philadelphia chromosome in CML guides diagnosis.
  8. Forensic medicine and paternity — DNA fingerprinting (VNTRs/STRs) identifies criminals, victims and parents; blood groups were used earlier.
  9. Blood transfusion and transplantation — ABO and Rh blood groups are inherited (Rh incompatibility → haemolytic disease of the newborn); HLA matching for organ and bone marrow transplants.
  10. Understanding common (multifactorial) diseases — diabetes, hypertension, coronary heart disease, asthma, schizophrenia; identifying genetic susceptibility allows lifestyle prevention in at-risk relatives.
  11. Explaining human variation and evolution — e.g. sickle cell trait (HbAS) gives protection against falciparum malaria — balanced polymorphism — explaining its high frequency in Nigeria.
  12. Prevention and public health — avoidance of consanguineous marriage, advice on advanced maternal age (Down syndrome risk), folic acid to reduce neural tube defects, avoiding mutagens/teratogens (radiation, drugs).

Conclusion

Genetics underlies diagnosis, risk prediction, prevention, drug safety and new treatments. A doctor who understands genetics can counsel families, interpret tests and practise preventive medicine — especially relevant for sickle cell disease in our environment.

Understand it (plain English) Genetics answers three questions patients ask: "Why did this happen?" (diagnosis), "Will it happen again?" (recurrence risk/counselling/prenatal tests) and "Will this drug harm me?" (pharmacogenetics). Everything else — screening, gene therapy, DNA fingerprinting — hangs off these.
