Describe the three Coats of Eyeball.

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants Name the three coats correctly (outer fibrous = sclera + cornea; middle vascular = uveal tract = choroid + ciliary body + iris; inner nervous = retina), give the structure and function of each part, a labelled horizontal section of the eyeball, the 10 layers of the retina, and one or two applied points (retinal detachment, glaucoma, corneal graft).

Introduction

The eyeball is a nearly spherical organ (about 24 mm in diameter) lodged in the orbit. Its wall is made of three coats (tunics), arranged from outside inwards:

  1. Outer fibrous coat — sclera (posterior 5/6) and cornea (anterior 1/6).
  2. Middle vascular coat (uveal tract / uvea) — choroid, ciliary body and iris.
  3. Inner nervous coat — retina.

The cavity enclosed by these coats contains the refractive media: aqueous humour, lens and vitreous body.

1. Outer fibrous coat

(a) Sclera
  • Tough, white, opaque coat of dense collagen fibres forming the posterior five-sixths of the eyeball ("white of the eye").
  • Posteriorly it is pierced by the optic nerve at the lamina cribrosa; it is continuous with the dural sheath of the optic nerve.
  • Gives attachment to the extraocular muscles.
  • Functions: protects the inner contents, maintains the shape of the eyeball (with intraocular pressure).
(b) Cornea
  • Transparent anterior one-sixth; more convex than the sclera. Junction of cornea and sclera = limbus (sclerocorneal junction), near which lies the canal of Schlemm.
  • Avascular; nourished by aqueous humour and tear film. Richly supplied by sensory nerves (ophthalmic division of trigeminal) → corneal reflex.
  • Five layers: epithelium, Bowman's membrane, substantia propria (stroma), Descemet's membrane, endothelium.
  • Functions: chief refracting surface of the eye (about 42 D of the total ~59–60 D); protection.

2. Middle vascular coat — uveal tract (uvea)

(a) Choroid
  • Thin, dark-brown, highly vascular and pigmented layer lining the inner surface of the sclera in the posterior part; extends forward to the ora serrata.
  • Functions: nourishes the outer layers of the retina (rods and cones); its pigment absorbs stray light and prevents internal reflection (like the black inside of a camera).
(b) Ciliary body
  • Thickened forward continuation of the choroid behind the iris. Contains the ciliary muscle (smooth muscle, parasympathetic supply via oculomotor nerve) and ciliary processes.
  • Functions: ciliary processes secrete aqueous humour; ciliary muscle produces accommodation (contraction relaxes the suspensory ligament → lens becomes more convex); suspends the lens via zonular fibres.
(c) Iris
  • Coloured, circular, contractile diaphragm in front of the lens with a central aperture — the pupil. Divides the space between cornea and lens into anterior and posterior chambers.
  • Two muscles: sphincter pupillae (circular; parasympathetic, CN III) → constricts pupil (miosis); dilator pupillae (radial; sympathetic) → dilates pupil (mydriasis).
  • Functions: regulates the amount of light entering the eye; increases depth of focus and reduces spherical/chromatic aberration in bright light and near vision.

3. Inner nervous coat — retina

  • Thin, delicate, light-sensitive membrane. Parts: optic (pars optica) part, which is photosensitive and ends at the ora serrata, and a non-nervous part (pars caeca) lining the ciliary body and iris.
  • Contains the photoreceptors: rods (~120 million; dim light) and cones (~6 million; bright light, colour, acuity).
  • Special areas: optic disc — where optic nerve fibres leave; no rods or cones → blind spot; macula lutea — yellow spot about 3 mm lateral (temporal) to the disc, with a central depression, the fovea centralis (only cones; point of sharpest vision).
  • Blood supply: inner layers from the central artery of the retina; outer layers (rods and cones) from the choroid by diffusion.

Applied physiology

  • Retinal detachment: the neural retina separates from the pigment epithelium (the plane between layers 1 and 2) → loss of vision in the affected area.
  • Corneal grafting succeeds well because the cornea is avascular (little immune rejection).
  • Uveitis — inflammation of the uveal tract; glaucoma — raised intraocular pressure damaging the optic disc (cupping).
  • Papilloedema — swelling of the optic disc in raised intracranial pressure (dural sheath continuous with sclera).

Conclusion

The fibrous coat protects and refracts, the vascular coat nourishes and controls the light and focus, and the nervous coat converts light into nerve impulses — together they make the eyeball a living camera.

Understand it (plain English) Think of a camera. The sclera is the hard body of the camera; the cornea is the front glass window. The choroid is the black paint inside that stops reflections, the iris is the aperture, and the ciliary muscle is the focusing ring. The retina is the film/sensor. Odd but important: light has to pass through all the retinal layers to reach the rods and cones, which face backwards against the pigment layer.
Memory trick Coats outside → in: "F-V-N" = Fibrous, Vascular, Nervous. Uvea parts front-to-back: "I Can Cry" = Iris, Ciliary body, Choroid. Mistakes that cost marks Calling the conjunctiva a coat of the eyeball (it is not). Forgetting that the cornea belongs to the outer coat. Saying the ciliary muscle is sympathetic (it is parasympathetic). Putting the fovea on the optic disc — they are separate; the disc is the blind spot.
