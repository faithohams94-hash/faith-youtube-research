List the bones contributing to the orbit and name five contents of the orbital cavity. [MK2010]

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants All seven bones (frontal, zygomatic, maxilla, sphenoid, ethmoid, lacrimal, palatine), ideally arranged wall by wall, plus any five correct contents (eyeball, extraocular muscles, optic nerve, ophthalmic artery/veins, lacrimal gland, ciliary ganglion, orbital fat…). A labelled "four walls" sketch lifts it to full marks.

Introduction

The orbits are a pair of bony cavities, one on either side of the root of the nose, which lodge and protect the eyeballs with their muscles, nerves and vessels. Each orbit is shaped like a four-sided pyramid: the base is the orbital opening (margin) on the face and the apex is at the medial end of the superior orbital fissure / optic canal. The medial walls of the two orbits are parallel; the lateral walls are at right angles to each other.

A. Bones forming the orbit (seven bones)

  1. Frontal
  2. Zygomatic
  3. Maxilla
  4. Sphenoid (greater wing, lesser wing, body)
  5. Ethmoid
  6. Lacrimal
  7. Palatine (orbital process)

Frontal, sphenoid and ethmoid are single (midline) bones shared by both orbits; zygomatic, maxilla, lacrimal and palatine are paired.

B. Contents of the orbital cavity (any five)

  1. Eyeball (occupies the anterior one-fifth / front part of the orbit) with the fascia bulbi (Tenon's capsule).
  2. Extraocular muscles: levator palpebrae superioris, four recti (superior, inferior, medial, lateral), superior and inferior obliques; plus involuntary muscles (superior and inferior tarsal, orbitalis).
  3. Optic nerve (with its meningeal sheaths).
  4. Nerves: oculomotor, trochlear, abducent; branches of ophthalmic nerve (lacrimal, frontal, nasociliary); zygomatic and infraorbital branches of maxillary nerve; sympathetic fibres.
  5. Ciliary ganglion (parasympathetic ganglion, between optic nerve and lateral rectus).
  6. Ophthalmic artery and its branches (incl. central artery of retina); infraorbital artery.
  7. Superior and inferior ophthalmic veins.
  8. Lacrimal gland (orbital part, in lacrimal fossa).
  9. Orbital fat, fascial sheaths and check ligaments; the suspensory ligament of Lockwood.

Clinical note

  • Blow-out fracture: a blow on the eye fractures the thin floor → orbital contents herniate into the maxillary sinus (inferior rectus may be trapped → diplopia on upward gaze; infraorbital nerve → numb cheek).
  • The paper-thin lamina papyracea allows ethmoidal sinus infection to spread into the orbit (orbital cellulitis).

Conclusion

Seven bones build the orbital pyramid — roof (frontal, lesser wing), lateral (zygomatic, greater wing), floor (maxilla, zygomatic, palatine), medial (maxilla, lacrimal, ethmoid, sphenoid body) — housing the eyeball and its motor, sensory and vascular apparatus.

Understand it (plain English) Think of the orbit as an ice-cream cone lying on its side, open end facing forward. The forehead bone (frontal) makes the ceiling, the cheek bone (zygomatic) the outer wall, the upper jaw (maxilla) the floor, and a thin "paper" bone (ethmoid) plus the tiny lacrimal bone the inner wall. Sphenoid sits at the back, around the point of the cone where the optic nerve leaves.
Memory trick Seven bones: "My Zany Friend Eats Lots of Sweet Pies" — Maxilla, Zygomatic, Frontal, Ethmoid, Lacrimal, Sphenoid, Palatine.
Medial wall from front to back: "MLES" — Maxilla, Lacrimal, Ethmoid, Sphenoid.
How to write this in the exam 6–8 minutes. One sentence definition (four-sided pyramid), the numbered list of seven bones, then the wall-by-wall table (this is what distinguishes a distinction script), then the two-square diagram, then five numbered contents. Write "any five" contents clearly numbered — the examiner ticks them.
Mistakes that cost marks Forgetting the palatine (tiny orbital process in the floor). Putting the greater wing in the roof — it is the lateral wall; the lesser wing is in the roof. Listing "eye" alone as five contents — list separate structures.
