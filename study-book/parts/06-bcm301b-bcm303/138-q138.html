Discuss the advantages and limitations of real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants Define PCR and real-time (quantitative, qPCR) PCR; explain the principle (fluorescence read every cycle, threshold cycle, Ct); name the detection chemistries (SYBR Green, TaqMan probe); then a clearly numbered list of advantages and limitations, with applications. The amplification plot is the marks-winning diagram.

1. Introduction / Definitions

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is an in-vitro technique (Kary Mullis, 1983) for the enzymatic amplification of a specific DNA segment using two oligonucleotide primers that flank the target, deoxynucleotides (dNTPs), Mg2+ and a heat-stable DNA polymerase (Taq polymerase from Thermus aquaticus). Each cycle has three steps — denaturation (~94 °C), annealing of primers (~50–60 °C) and extension (72 °C) — and the product theoretically doubles each cycle (2n copies after n cycles).

Real-time PCR (quantitative PCR, qPCR) is a modification in which the accumulation of PCR product is measured during each cycle, as it happens, by a fluorescent signal, instead of only at the end on an agarose gel. When combined with a reverse-transcription step (RT-qPCR) it quantifies RNA (e.g., mRNA expression, RNA viruses).

2. Principle

  1. A fluorescent reporter is included in the reaction; fluorescence increases in proportion to the amount of double-stranded product.
  2. An instrument (thermocycler with an optical detector) reads fluorescence at the end of every cycle and plots fluorescence against cycle number — the amplification plot.
  3. The plot has four phases: baseline → exponential → linear → plateau (reagents exhausted).
  4. The threshold cycle (Ct, Cq) is the cycle at which fluorescence first rises above a set threshold. Ct is inversely proportional to the log of the starting amount of target: the more template present, the earlier (lower) the Ct. Each 1-cycle difference ≈ 2-fold difference in starting template.
  5. Quantification is done in the exponential phase, either absolute (standard curve of known copy numbers) or relative (compared with a reference/housekeeping gene, the ΔΔCt method).

4. Advantages of real-time PCR

  1. Quantitative — measures the starting amount of DNA/RNA (copy number, viral load, gene expression), whereas conventional end-point PCR is only qualitative/semi-quantitative.
  2. Wide dynamic range — accurate over about 7–8 log units of template concentration, because measurement is made in the exponential phase, not at the plateau.
  3. High sensitivity — can detect very few (theoretically single) copies of a target.
  4. High specificity — with probes, the signal comes only from the intended sequence; melt curves confirm product identity with SYBR Green.
  5. Speed — results in 1–2 hours; no post-PCR gel electrophoresis, staining or blotting.
  6. Closed-tube system — tubes are not opened after amplification, so less carry-over contamination and less exposure to ethidium bromide.
  7. Multiplexing — several targets (each with a different coloured probe) in one tube, including an internal control.
  8. Automation and reproducibility — high throughput (96–384 wells), computer analysis, objective numerical results.
  9. RT-qPCR for RNA — measures mRNA levels (gene expression) and RNA viruses (HIV, hepatitis C, SARS-CoV-2).
  10. Genotyping — detection of point mutations/SNPs (e.g., sickle-cell mutation) with allele-specific probes or melt-curve analysis.

5. Limitations

  1. Cost — expensive instrument, fluorescent probes and reagents; needs trained personnel and stable electricity (relevant in our setting).
  2. Sequence must be known — primers and probes can only be designed for known targets; it cannot discover unknown sequences.
  3. Non-specific signal with SYBR Green — primer-dimers and non-specific products give false fluorescence.
  4. No information on product size or sequence beyond the melt curve (needs gel or sequencing for that).
  5. Dependence on amplification efficiency — inhibitors in the sample (heme, heparin, ethanol, phenol) and differences in efficiency between targets distort quantification; standard curves and reference genes are required.
  6. Normalisation problems — relative quantification assumes the reference (housekeeping) gene is constant, which is not always true.
  7. Limited multiplexing — restricted by the number of fluorescent dyes the optical system can distinguish.
  8. Contamination still possible — extremely sensitive, so any contaminating template gives a false positive.
  9. Detects nucleic acid, not viability — DNA from dead organisms is also amplified; a positive result does not always mean active infection.
  10. Short amplicons are preferred (about 70–200 bp); long targets amplify poorly.

6. Applications (brief)

Viral load monitoring (HIV, HBV, HCV); diagnosis of infections (tuberculosis, SARS-CoV-2); gene expression studies; detection of mutations and SNPs; quantification of oncogene amplification and minimal residual disease (e.g., BCR-ABL in CML); prenatal diagnosis; food/GMO testing.

7. Conclusion

Real-time PCR converts PCR from a yes/no test into a fast, sensitive, closed-tube, quantitative assay based on the Ct value; its main drawbacks are cost, the need for known sequences, and sensitivity to inhibitors and contamination.

Understand it (plain English) Imagine three photocopiers all doubling pages every minute. The one that started with the most pages fills the room to a "notice it" level first. Real-time PCR simply watches for when each tube "lights up" — the earlier it lights up (lower Ct), the more target it started with. Conventional PCR only looks at the end, when every tube is full (plateau) and they all look the same.
