How does DNA mutation relate to DNA repair and gene therapy?

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants Three definitions (mutation, DNA repair, gene therapy) and — most importantly — the link: damage that escapes repair becomes a mutation; defective repair genes raise the mutation rate (cancer, XP); mutations cause genetic disease; gene therapy supplies or corrects the faulty gene. Types of mutation, repair mechanisms and gene-therapy strategies, each briefly, with an example.

1. Definitions

  • Mutation — a permanent, heritable change in the nucleotide sequence of DNA.
  • DNA repair — the set of enzymatic systems that recognise and correct damage or mispairing in DNA before it becomes fixed as a mutation.
  • Gene therapy — introduction of a normal (functional) gene, or correction/silencing of a defective gene, in a patient's cells in order to treat a genetic disease.

b) Causes (mutagens)
  1. Spontaneous: replication errors; depurination; deamination (C → U, 5-methyl-C → T); tautomeric shifts.
  2. Physical: UV light → thymine (pyrimidine) dimers; ionising radiation (X-rays, γ-rays) → single/double-strand breaks.
  3. Chemical: base analogues (5-bromouracil), alkylating agents, nitrous acid (deaminates), intercalating agents (acridine, ethidium → frameshift), aflatoxin.
  4. Biological: viruses and transposons inserting into genes.

4. The relationship: mutation ↔ repair

  1. Repair prevents mutation. DNA is damaged thousands of times per cell per day; almost all damage is corrected. Only damage that escapes repair, or is repaired wrongly, is copied at the next replication and becomes a fixed mutation.
  2. Damage vs mutation. A damaged base or mismatch is recognisable (abnormal structure). After replication, both daughter strands carry a normal-looking but wrong base pair — the repair enzymes can no longer "see" it. So repair must act before replication.
  3. Mutations in repair genes themselves cause a mutator phenotype: the mutation rate of all other genes rises — hence the high cancer risk in xeroderma pigmentosum, HNPCC, BRCA carriers, Bloom syndrome and Fanconi anaemia.
  4. Error-prone repair (e.g., the bacterial SOS response) lets the cell survive heavy damage at the cost of introducing mutations.
  5. Accumulation of unrepaired mutations in oncogenes and tumour-suppressor genes (e.g., p53, the "guardian of the genome", which halts the cell cycle for repair or triggers apoptosis) leads to cancer and contributes to ageing.
  6. Mutation is not always harmful: it supplies variation for evolution and diversity (e.g., antibody genes).

5. Mutation ↔ gene therapy

  1. Once a disease-causing mutation is inherited, cellular repair cannot correct it — every cell carries it. Gene therapy attempts to correct the consequence at the gene level.
  2. Types: somatic gene therapy (body cells; not inherited — the only type ethically permitted) vs germline (sperm/ova/embryo; inherited — not permitted in humans).
  3. Approaches: ex vivo (cells removed, e.g., bone-marrow stem cells, transfected, returned) and in vivo (vector given directly, e.g., aerosol to airway in cystic fibrosis).
  4. Strategies: (a) gene addition/augmentation — insert a normal copy (for recessive loss-of-function diseases); (b) gene correction/editing at the exact site; (c) gene silencing — antisense oligonucleotides or RNA interference against a harmful dominant gene; (d) suicide genes / immune-stimulating genes in cancer.
  5. Vectors: retroviruses (integrate; dividing cells), adenoviruses (non-dividing cells, do not integrate), adeno-associated virus, liposomes, naked DNA.
  6. Example: the first approved gene therapy (1990) — adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency causing severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID): the patient's T-lymphocytes were given the normal ADA gene using a retroviral vector. Candidate diseases: cystic fibrosis, haemophilia, DMD, thalassaemia, sickle-cell disease.
  7. Problems: immune reaction to vector, short-lived expression, insertional mutagenesis (the vector inserting next to an oncogene — leukaemia occurred in some X-SCID trials: gene therapy itself can cause a mutation), cost and ethics.

6. Conclusion

Mutation is the outcome of DNA damage that escapes repair; DNA repair is the cell's defence that keeps mutation rate low, and its failure leads to cancer and genetic disease; gene therapy is the medical attempt to correct the effects of mutations that the cell can no longer repair.

Understand it (plain English) Think of DNA as a book being photocopied. A coffee stain (damage) can be cleaned off by the proof-reader (repair). If the copier copies the stained page before cleaning, the error is now printed in every copy — that is a mutation; the proof-reader cannot tell it is wrong any more. If the proof-reader itself is faulty (XP, HNPCC), errors pile up — cancer. Gene therapy is like pasting a correct page into the book.
Memory trick Repair systems = "My BestNephew Does Damage": Mismatch, Base excision, Nucleotide excision, Direct, Double-strand break. Diseases: XP = eXcision (NER); HNPCC = misHatch (mismatch); BRCA = Breaks.
How to write this in the exam Start with the three definitions in one block. Draw the concept map early (it shows you understand the relationship — the whole point of the question). Then: types of mutation table → repair table → the "relationship" points → gene therapy (types, vectors, ADA-SCID example). Use the sentence "DNA repair acts on damage before replication; mutation is damage fixed by replication".
Mistakes that cost marks Writing three separate mini-essays without ever linking them. Saying germline gene therapy is used in humans. Confusing transition with transversion. Giving photolyase as a human enzyme (humans rely on nucleotide excision repair for thymine dimers).
