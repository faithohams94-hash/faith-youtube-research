A. Organization of the human genome

  1. Definition: the genome is the complete set of DNA of an organism. The haploid human nuclear genome is about 3 × 109 base pairs on 23 chromosomes (diploid cells: 46).
  2. Genes: only about 20,000–25,000 protein-coding genes; coding exons form less than 2% of the genome. Genes are split — exons (expressed) interrupted by introns (removed by splicing). Also present: promoters, enhancers, genes for rRNA, tRNA and other non-coding RNAs, and pseudogenes (non-functional copies).
  3. Unique (single-copy) DNA — most structural genes.
  4. Repetitive DNA:
    • Highly repetitive — short sequences repeated in tandem millions of times (satellite DNA at centromeres; telomeric TTAGGG repeats).
    • Moderately repetitive — interspersed repeats: SINEs (e.g., Alu family, ~300 bp, about a million copies) and LINEs (e.g., L1, ~6 kb); also multi-copy genes for rRNA, tRNA and histones.
    • Mini- and microsatellites (VNTRs, STRs) — tandem repeats of variable number; highly polymorphic → used as markers.
  5. Gene families — related genes clustered, e.g., α- and β-globin gene clusters.
  6. Packaging (chromatin): DNA (~2 m per cell) is wound ~1.75 turns (~146 bp) around a histone octamer (H2A, H2B, H3, H4 × 2) to form a nucleosome (10 nm "beads on a string"; linker DNA with H1) → 30 nm fibre (solenoid) → looped domains on a protein scaffold → metaphase chromosome. Euchromatin (loose, transcriptionally active) vs heterochromatin (condensed, inactive).
  7. Mitochondrial genome: circular, 16,569 bp, 37 genes (13 polypeptides of oxidative phosphorylation, 22 tRNAs, 2 rRNAs); maternally inherited, no introns, no histones.

B. Gene (DNA) marker protocols

A genetic marker is a DNA sequence with a known location that varies between individuals (polymorphic) and can be tracked; used for linkage of disease genes, DNA fingerprinting, paternity and forensic testing.

MarkerNatureProtocol
RFLP (restriction fragment length polymorphism)A base change creates or destroys a restriction site → fragments of different lengthExtract DNA → digest with restriction endonuclease → agarose gel electrophoresis → Southern blot to nitrocellulose → hybridise with labelled probe → autoradiography. (Example: MstII site lost in HbS gene.)
VNTR (minisatellites)Tandem repeats of 10–100 bp; number of repeats variesRestriction digest + Southern blot, or PCR with flanking primers → electrophoresis; basis of classical DNA fingerprinting (Jeffreys)
STR (microsatellites)Repeats of 2–6 bp (e.g., (CA)n)PCR with fluorescent flanking primers → capillary electrophoresis; standard forensic/paternity panels
SNP (single-nucleotide polymorphism)Single-base variation, the commonest polymorphismPCR + allele-specific probe, real-time PCR, sequencing, or DNA micro-arrays

C. The genetic code (brief)

The genetic code is the relationship between the sequence of bases in mRNA and the sequence of amino acids in protein. It is read in triplets (codons): 43 = 64 codons — 61 sense codons for 20 amino acids and 3 stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA); AUG is the start codon (methionine). Features: triplet, universal (exceptions in mitochondria, e.g., UGA = Trp), degenerate (most amino acids have more than one codon; only Met and Trp have one), unambiguous (one codon → one amino acid), non-overlapping, comma-less (read continuously from a fixed start), with a wobble at the third base. (Full note: Q145.)

D. Activities at a typical DNA replication fork

ProteinActivity at the fork
Helicase (DnaB in E. coli)Unwinds the double helix by breaking H-bonds; uses ATP
Single-strand binding proteins (SSB; RPA in eukaryotes)Coat single strands, prevent re-annealing and nuclease attack
Topoisomerase (DNA gyrase = topo II in bacteria)Cuts and rejoins DNA ahead of the fork to relieve positive supercoils
PrimaseSynthesises short RNA primer (DNA polymerase cannot start a chain de novo — needs a free 3′-OH)
DNA polymerase III (prok.); pol α (primer extension), δ (lagging), ε (leading) (euk.)Adds dNTPs to 3′-OH, 5′→3′ only; 3′→5′ exonuclease proof-reading; sliding clamp (β-clamp / PCNA) gives processivity
DNA polymerase I (prok.) / RNase H + FEN1 (euk.)Removes RNA primers (5′→3′ exonuclease) and fills the gaps
DNA ligaseSeals nicks between Okazaki fragments (phosphodiester bond; NAD+ in bacteria, ATP in humans)
Telomerase (eukaryote, chromosome ends)Reverse transcriptase carrying its own RNA template; extends 3′ end of telomeres (end-replication problem)

Key principles: replication is semi-conservative (Meselson–Stahl), bidirectional from origins (one origin in E. coli, thousands in human chromosomes), semi-discontinuous (leading continuous, lagging as Okazaki fragments of ~1000–2000 nt in bacteria and ~100–200 nt in eukaryotes), and highly accurate because of proof-reading.

Conclusion

The human genome is large, mostly non-coding and packed into chromatin; its polymorphic repeats serve as markers; the triplet genetic code translates its genes; and the replication fork, with helicase, primase, polymerases and ligase, copies it faithfully at every cell division.

Understand it (plain English) DNA polymerase can only "write" in one direction (5′→3′) and needs a starter (primer). At a fork the two template strands run in opposite directions, so one new strand is written smoothly towards the fork (leading), while the other has to be written backwards in short pieces each time a bit more template is exposed (lagging) — then the pieces are glued by ligase.
Memory trick Order of events at the fork: "Hungry Students Take Pizza Pieces Liberally" — Helicase, SSB, Topoisomerase, Primase, Polymerase, Ligase. Markers by size: "VNTR is Very big, STR is Small, SNP is Single".
How to write this in the exam Four headings A–D, about 6 minutes each. Draw the fork diagram first in part D and then the enzyme table. In part A, quote three numbers: 3 × 109 bp, ~20,000–25,000 genes, <2% coding. In part B, write the RFLP protocol as a flow: digest → gel → Southern blot → probe → autoradiograph.
Mistakes that cost marks Drawing the lagging strand fragments pointing towards the fork. Forgetting the primer (polymerase cannot start a new chain). Saying helicase relieves supercoiling (that is topoisomerase). Saying the human genome has 100,000 genes (old estimate).
