Introduction and definitions

  • Nutrition — the science of food, the nutrients in it, and how the body ingests, digests, absorbs, transports, utilises and excretes them for growth, maintenance, repair and reproduction.
  • Nutrient — a chemical substance in food used by the body: macronutrients (carbohydrate, protein, fat) and micronutrients (vitamins, minerals), plus water and dietary fibre.
  • Balanced diet — one that supplies all nutrients in the correct amounts and proportions to meet requirements, with a small margin for stress. Roughly 50–60% of energy from carbohydrate, 10–15% from protein, 20–30% from fat.
  • Health (WHO) — a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.

Good nutrition therefore ensures: (1) normal growth and development in children; (2) maintenance and repair of tissues; (3) adequate energy for BMR, physical activity and thermogenesis; (4) a strong immune system (protein, Zn, vitamins A, C); (5) successful pregnancy and lactation (folate, iron, iodine, calcium); (6) mental development (iodine, iron, protein in infancy); (7) healthy ageing and longevity.

B. Role of nutrition in disease

1. Under-nutrition (deficiency diseases)
  1. Protein-energy malnutrition — kwashiorkor (protein deficit; oedema, fatty liver, hypoalbuminaemia) and marasmus (overall energy deficit; severe wasting).
  2. Vitamin deficiencies — thiamine (beriberi, Wernicke–Korsakoff), niacin (pellagra), vitamin C (scurvy), vitamin D (rickets/osteomalacia), vitamin A (night blindness, xerophthalmia — a leading cause of preventable childhood blindness), folate/B12 (megaloblastic anaemia, NTDs), vitamin K (bleeding in newborns).
  3. Mineral deficiencies — iron (microcytic anaemia), iodine (goitre, cretinism), zinc (growth failure, hypogonadism), calcium (osteoporosis), fluoride (caries).
  4. Malnutrition–infection cycle — undernutrition lowers immunity → infections (measles, diarrhoea, TB) → anorexia, malabsorption and increased catabolism → worse malnutrition.
2. Over-nutrition and imbalance (diseases of affluence)
  1. Obesity — energy intake > expenditure; BMI ≥ 30. Leads to insulin resistance.
  2. Type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia and atherosclerosis/coronary heart disease — diets high in saturated fat, trans fat, cholesterol and refined sugar; low in fibre and PUFA.
  3. Hypertension — excess sodium, alcohol, obesity.
  4. Cancers — low fibre and high red/processed meat (colon cancer); aflatoxin-contaminated foods (liver cancer).
  5. Toxicity — hypervitaminosis A (raised intracranial pressure, teratogenic) and D (hypercalcaemia, metastatic calcification).
3. Inborn errors and food intolerance

Phenylketonuria (restrict phenylalanine), galactosaemia (remove galactose), lactose intolerance (lactase deficiency), coeliac disease (gluten), favism (G6PD deficiency + fava beans).

4. Nutrition in the management of disease (therapeutic diets)
  • Diabetes — controlled energy, complex carbohydrates, high fibre (low glycaemic index).
  • Chronic kidney disease — restricted protein, potassium, phosphate.
  • Liver failure — protein adjusted to avoid hepatic encephalopathy.
  • Hypertension/heart disease — low salt, low saturated fat.
  • Increased requirements in fever, burns, surgery, pregnancy, lactation and growth.

Conclusion

Nutrition is the foundation of health: an adequate, balanced diet sustains growth, repair, immunity and reproduction, while both deficiency and excess cause disease. In Nigeria the "double burden" — PEM and micronutrient deficiency alongside rising obesity and diabetes — makes nutritional biochemistry central to medicine.

Understand it (plain English) Kris, picture a see-saw. Too little food and the body breaks down muscle, cannot fight infection, and the child stops growing. Too much — especially sugar and fat — and the body stores the surplus as fat, insulin stops working well, arteries clog. Health lives in the middle.
