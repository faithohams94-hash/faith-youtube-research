Part 1 — Biochemistry of eating disorders

Eating disorders are psychiatric conditions with abnormal eating behaviour that seriously disturbs nutrition and metabolism. Main types:

DisorderBehaviourBody weight
Anorexia nervosaSelf-imposed starvation, intense fear of weight gain, distorted body image; mainly adolescent girlsVery low (BMI often < 17.5)
Bulimia nervosaBinge eating followed by compensatory self-induced vomiting, laxative/diuretic abuse, fastingUsually normal
Binge-eating disorderRecurrent binges without compensationOverweight/obese
A. Metabolic adaptation to starvation (anorexia nervosa)
  1. Early (first 1–2 days) — insulin ↓, glucagon ↑ → liver glycogenolysis; glycogen exhausted within ~24 h.
  2. Gluconeogenesis from muscle amino acids (alanine, glutamine), lactate and glycerol → muscle wasting, negative nitrogen balance.
  3. Lipolysis ↑ (hormone-sensitive lipase) → free fatty acids → β-oxidation → excess acetyl-CoA → ketone bodies (ketosis, ketonuria). After 2–3 weeks the brain uses ketone bodies for much of its energy, sparing protein.
  4. Hormonal: ↓ T3 (more inactive reverse T3) → ↓ BMR, bradycardia, hypothermia; ↓ leptin → ↓ GnRH/LH/FSH → amenorrhoea, ↓ oestrogen → osteoporosis; ↑ cortisol and growth hormone (with ↓ IGF-1).
  5. Blood chemistry: hypoglycaemia (late), raised cholesterol (paradoxical), low albumin only in late stages, anaemia, leucopenia, low zinc.
B. Electrolyte disturbances (bulimia and purging anorexia)
  • Vomiting loses HCl → hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis with hypokalaemia (K+ lost in vomit and urine via secondary hyperaldosteronism) → cardiac arrhythmias, muscle weakness.
  • Laxative abuse → loss of HCO3− and K+ → metabolic acidosis, hypokalaemia; hypomagnesaemia.
  • Raised serum amylase (salivary gland enlargement); dental enamel erosion by gastric acid.
C. Refeeding syndrome

When feeding restarts, insulin surges → cells take up glucose with phosphate, K+ and Mg2+ (phosphate is used to make ATP and phosphorylated intermediates) → dangerous hypophosphataemia, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, thiamine deficiency → heart failure, arrhythmias. Hence refeeding is slow.

D. Binge-eating / obesity

Excess energy → lipogenesis, obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia, fatty liver.

Part 2 — Nutritional assessment in children ("ABCD")

  1. A — Anthropometry (most useful, simple, cheap):
    • Weight-for-age — overall under-nutrition (underweight); basis of the Wellcome and Gomez classifications.
    • Height-for-age — low = stunting (chronic malnutrition).
    • Weight-for-height — low = wasting (acute malnutrition); < −3 Z-score = severe acute malnutrition (SAM).
    • Mid-upper-arm circumference (MUAC) — 1–5 years: > 13.5 cm normal, 12.5–13.5 borderline, < 12.5 cm moderate, < 11.5 cm severe. Remains fairly constant between 1 and 5 years, so age need not be known (Shakir's tape).
    • Head and chest circumference — at birth head > chest; chest overtakes head by about 1 year; failure suggests malnutrition.
    • Skinfold thickness (triceps, subscapular) — fat stores.
    • Growth chart (Road-to-Health card) — serial weights; a flat or falling curve is the earliest sign.
  2. B — Biochemical tests: serum albumin (< 3 g/dL in kwashiorkor), total protein, prealbumin/transferrin (short half-life), haemoglobin, serum ferritin, blood glucose, electrolytes; urinary creatinine–height index (muscle mass); urinary hydroxyproline index; vitamin levels (retinol, folate, B12), zinc.
  3. C — Clinical examination: hair (sparse, depigmented, "flag sign"), skin (flaky-paint dermatosis, phrynoderma), eyes (Bitot's spots, xerosis), mouth (angular stomatitis, glossitis), oedema, hepatomegaly, rickety changes, wasting, behaviour (apathy, irritability).
  4. D — Dietary assessment: 24-hour recall, food frequency questionnaire, weighed food intake; feeding history (breast-feeding, weaning). Plus vital statistics (infant and under-5 mortality) at community level.

Part 3 — Wellcome classification of PEM

Proposed by the Wellcome Trust working party (1970). Uses two simple criteria: weight-for-age as % of the expected (Harvard 50th centile) standard, and presence or absence of oedema.

Weight-for-age (% of standard)Oedema absentOedema present
60–80%UnderweightKwashiorkor
< 60%MarasmusMarasmic kwashiorkor

Merits: simple, needs only a scale and the child's age; separates oedematous from non-oedematous PEM. Limitations: needs accurate age (often unknown); oedema raises weight and masks wasting; ignores height, so cannot separate stunting from wasting (Waterlow's classification does that); > 80% treated as normal.

Conclusion

Eating disorders reproduce the biochemistry of starvation plus purge-related electrolyte loss; children's nutritional status is judged by ABCD with anthropometry first; and the Wellcome scheme classifies PEM by weight-for-age and oedema into four groups.

Understand it (plain English) In anorexia the body behaves exactly like a famine victim: it burns glycogen, then muscle, then fat (making ketones), and turns the "thermostat" (T3) down to save fuel. In bulimia the big danger is losing stomach acid and potassium by vomiting. For Wellcome, just remember two questions: "How heavy is the child compared with normal?" and "Is there oedema?"
Memory trick Wellcome: "60–80 Under-Kwash, below 60 Mar-MarKwash" — top row Underweight/Kwashiorkor, bottom row Marasmus/Marasmic-kwashiorkor; oedema always on the right. Assessment: "ABCD" — Anthropometry, Biochemical, Clinical, Dietary.
How to write this in the exam "Examine" three topics → split time equally (~8 min each). Draw the Wellcome grid — it is almost a full mark by itself. For eating disorders, use the headings: starvation adaptation → electrolytes → hormones → refeeding.
Mistakes that cost marks Swapping marasmus and kwashiorkor in the grid. Writing < 70% instead of < 60%. Saying bulimics are always thin (most have normal weight). Calling vomiting-induced alkalosis "acidosis".
