Discuss the classification of drugs under: legal classification; pharmacological effect; mechanism of action; chemical nature.

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants A definition of a drug, then four separate headings, each with 4–6 classes and a named example drug for every class. Examples with their targets (e.g. aspirin → cyclo-oxygenase) earn the marks under "mechanism of action".

Introduction / Definition

A drug is any substance or product used, or intended to be used, to change or investigate physiological systems or disease states for the benefit of the recipient: to diagnose, prevent or treat disease. Most drugs are xenobiotics (compounds foreign to the body). The body handles them by absorption, distribution, biotransformation and excretion. Drugs can be classified in several ways. Each method answers a different question:

  • Legal: who may supply it?
  • Pharmacological effect: what does it do in the body?
  • Mechanism of action: at what molecular target does it act?
  • Chemical nature: what is it made of?

1. Legal classification (by how a drug may be supplied)

Drug laws decide who may sell, prescribe and possess each drug, based on how safe it is and how likely it is to be misused. In Nigeria this is regulated by NAFDAC, the Pharmacists Council of Nigeria and (for narcotics) the NDLEA.

2. Classification by pharmacological (therapeutic) effect

  1. Analgesics: opioid (morphine, codeine) and non-opioid/NSAIDs (aspirin, ibuprofen); antipyretics (paracetamol).
  2. Antimicrobials: antibacterial (penicillins, tetracyclines), antimalarial (artemisinins, chloroquine), antifungal (ketoconazole), antiviral (zidovudine), anthelmintic (albendazole).
  3. Cardiovascular drugs: antihypertensives (amlodipine, lisinopril), antiarrhythmics, cardiac glycosides (digoxin), anti-anginals (nitrates).
  4. CNS drugs: sedative-hypnotics (diazepam), anticonvulsants (phenytoin, phenobarbitone), antidepressants, antipsychotics, general and local anaesthetics.
  5. Drugs acting on blood: anticoagulants (heparin, warfarin/dicoumarol), haematinics (ferrous sulphate, folic acid).
  6. Endocrine and metabolic drugs: hypoglycaemics (insulin, metformin), thyroid drugs, corticosteroids, hypolipidaemics (statins).
  7. Others: diuretics (furosemide), bronchodilators (salbutamol), antiemetics, antacids/antiulcer drugs, cytotoxic anticancer drugs (methotrexate, 5-fluorouracil).

3. Classification by mechanism of action (molecular target)

4. Classification by chemical nature

  1. Alkaloids: nitrogen-containing basic plant products, e.g. morphine and codeine (opium), quinine (cinchona bark), atropine (belladonna), caffeine, nicotine.
  2. Glycosides: a sugar linked to a non-sugar aglycone, e.g. digoxin and digitoxin (cardiac glycosides, from Digitalis).
  3. Steroids: have the cyclopentanoperhydrophenanthrene nucleus, e.g. prednisolone, hydrocortisone, oral contraceptives (ethinyl oestradiol), anabolic steroids.
  4. Peptides and proteins: insulin, oxytocin, vasopressin, heparin (a glycosaminoglycan), monoclonal antibodies, vaccines.
  5. Synthetic organic compounds (by chemical group): β-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins), sulphonamides, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, salicylates (aspirin), catecholamine derivatives (salbutamol), coumarins (warfarin), aminoglycosides.
  6. Lipids and vitamins: fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, K), prostaglandin analogues (misoprostol).
  7. Inorganic compounds: ferrous sulphate, lithium carbonate, magnesium hydroxide, potassium chloride, iodine.
  8. Nucleic acid analogues: zidovudine, acyclovir, 5-fluorouracil.

Why chemical nature matters biochemically: it decides lipid solubility, which controls absorption, crossing of the blood–brain barrier and the route of metabolism. Lipophilic drugs need phase I/II biotransformation before they can be excreted. Peptides are digested in the gut, so they must be injected (e.g. insulin).

Conclusion

One drug can sit in all four schemes. For example, morphine is a controlled drug (legal), an analgesic (effect), a μ-opioid agonist (mechanism) and an alkaloid (chemical nature). Each scheme serves a different purpose: regulation, therapeutics, pharmacology and chemistry.

Understand it (plain English) The four schemes are four ways of sorting the same objects. Legal: "who is allowed to hand it out?" Effect: "what does the patient notice?" Mechanism: "which molecule in the body does it grab?" Chemical: "what family of molecules is it?" Kris, if you can place morphine, aspirin and insulin in all four, you understand the question.
Memory trick Legal = "P-P-O-C-I": Prescription, Pharmacy, OTC, Controlled, Illicit.
Mechanism = "E-R-I-T-A-P" ("Every Receptor Is Targeted At Pharmacy"): Enzymes, Receptors (agonist/antagonist), Ion channels, Transporters, Antimetabolites, Physicochemical.
Chemical = "A-G-S-P-I" ("All Good Students Pass Interviews"): Alkaloids, Glycosides, Steroids, Peptides, Inorganic (plus synthetics).
How to write this in the exam About 25 min. Write the definition (2 lines), draw Fig 221.1 first as your "map", then use four headings in the order the question lists them. Use a table for mechanism of action, because that section carries the biochemistry marks. Give at least one example for every class. End with the morphine example as a one-line conclusion.
Mistakes that cost marks (1) Mixing up effect and mechanism. "Antihypertensive" is an effect; "ACE inhibitor" is a mechanism. (2) Listing classes with no example drugs. (3) Calling paracetamol an NSAID. It is an analgesic-antipyretic with very weak anti-inflammatory action. (4) Forgetting controlled drugs under the legal heading.
