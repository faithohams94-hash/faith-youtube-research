Antibiotic resistance is a bane on effective healthcare delivery. Explain succinctly.

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants A definition, then the biochemical mechanisms of resistance (enzyme inactivation, target alteration, reduced permeability, efflux, bypass), the genetic basis (mutation, plasmids, transposons, conjugation/transformation/transduction), the causes, why it is a "bane" (impact on healthcare) and prevention. "Succinctly" means tight numbered points.

Definition

Antibiotic resistance is the ability of a micro-organism to survive and multiply in a concentration of an antibiotic that would normally kill or inhibit it, so the drug no longer cures the infection. Resistance can be intrinsic (natural, e.g. Gram-negative bacteria are resistant to vancomycin because their outer membrane keeps it out) or acquired (by mutation or by gaining genes). Organisms resistant to several drug classes are called multidrug-resistant (MDR).

Biochemical mechanisms of resistance

  1. Enzymatic inactivation of the drug: β-lactamases hydrolyse the β-lactam ring of penicillins and cephalosporins. Extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBL) and carbapenemases destroy even newer drugs. Aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (acetyl-, phospho- and adenylyl-transferases) and chloramphenicol acetyltransferase inactivate those drugs.
  2. Alteration of the target site: the drug can no longer bind its target. Examples: altered penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a, coded by the mecA gene) in MRSA; methylation of 23S rRNA (macrolide resistance); point mutations in DNA gyrase (quinolones) or RNA polymerase (rifampicin in TB); D-Ala-D-Lac instead of D-Ala-D-Ala in cell-wall precursors (vancomycin-resistant enterococci).
  3. Decreased permeability: loss or mutation of outer-membrane porins in Gram-negative bacteria reduces drug entry.
  4. Active efflux: membrane pumps (some driven by the proton gradient, others ATP-binding cassette pumps) expel the drug, e.g. tetracycline efflux. Multidrug efflux pumps give cross-resistance to many classes.
  5. Metabolic bypass / overproduction of target: production of a drug-insensitive dihydropteroate synthase (sulphonamides) or dihydrofolate reductase (trimethoprim), or excess PABA.
  6. Biofilm formation: bacteria in a polysaccharide matrix (e.g. on catheters) are shielded from drugs and the immune system.

Genetic basis: how resistance arises and spreads

  1. Chromosomal mutation (vertical transmission). Spontaneous mutants are selected when antibiotics kill the sensitive cells, e.g. M. tuberculosis resistant to rifampicin and isoniazid (MDR-TB).
  2. Horizontal gene transfer of resistance genes on R-plasmids, transposons ("jumping genes") and integrons:
    • Conjugation: plasmid transfer through a sex pilus. This is the most important route, and one plasmid can carry several resistance genes (MDR).
    • Transformation: uptake of naked DNA from dead bacteria.
    • Transduction: transfer by bacteriophages (important in Staph. aureus).

Causes / promoting factors

  1. Over-prescription and irrational use, e.g. antibiotics for viral colds and diarrhoea.
  2. Self-medication and over-the-counter sale of antibiotics without prescription, which is common in our setting.
  3. Incomplete courses and sub-therapeutic doses, which select resistant mutants.
  4. Substandard and counterfeit drugs with low active ingredient.
  5. Heavy use in livestock and poultry as growth promoters.
  6. Poor infection control in hospitals (hand hygiene, sterilisation), poor sanitation and overcrowding.
  7. Lack of culture-and-sensitivity testing, which leads to "blind" broad-spectrum use.

Why it is a "bane" on healthcare delivery (impact)

  1. Treatment failure: common infections (pneumonia, typhoid, UTI, gonorrhoea, neonatal sepsis) become hard or impossible to treat. This raises morbidity and mortality.
  2. Longer hospital stay and more complications. Resistant organisms spread to other patients (nosocomial infection).
  3. Higher cost: second- and third-line drugs (carbapenems, linezolid, MDR-TB regimens) are expensive, more toxic and often unavailable.
  4. Threat to modern medicine: surgery, caesarean section, cancer chemotherapy, organ transplantation and care of premature babies all depend on effective prophylactic antibiotics.
  5. Public-health threat: MRSA, ESBL-producing E. coli/Klebsiella, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae, MDR/XDR-TB and drug-resistant typhoid. The WHO lists antimicrobial resistance among the top global health threats.

Prevention and control

  1. Antibiotic stewardship: right drug, right dose, right duration. Guide therapy by culture and sensitivity.
  2. Ban over-the-counter antibiotic sales; enforce drug-quality regulation (NAFDAC).
  3. Infection prevention: hand washing, sterilisation, isolation of resistant cases.
  4. Vaccination (fewer infections means less antibiotic use), clean water and sanitation.
  5. Combination therapy where appropriate (e.g. TB), and β-lactamase inhibitors (clavulanic acid with amoxicillin).
  6. Surveillance, public education, reduced use in agriculture, and research into new antibiotics.

Conclusion

Bacteria resist antibiotics by destroying them, changing their targets, keeping them out or pumping them out. These traits spread rapidly by plasmids under the selection pressure of misuse. The result is failed treatment, higher mortality and cost, and a threat to all of modern medicine. Rational use and infection control are the cure.

Understand it (plain English) Every antibiotic course is a "test" that kills sensitive bacteria and leaves the rare resistant ones to take over. Bacteria then share their resistance genes on plasmids, like passing exam answers around a class. Their defence tricks are like a thief beating a lock: break the key (β-lactamase), change the lock (altered target), block the door (porins), or throw the key back out (efflux pump).
