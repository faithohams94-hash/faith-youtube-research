Introduction / Definitions

Xenobiotics (Greek xenos, stranger) are compounds foreign to the body: drugs, food additives, pesticides, pollutants, carcinogens and alcohol. Endogenous compounds such as bilirubin, steroids and bile acids are handled by the same enzymes. The body changes these mostly lipophilic compounds, mainly in the liver (also the intestine, kidney, lungs and skin), into polar, water-soluble products that can be excreted in urine or bile. This is called biotransformation, or "detoxification". The term detoxification is not exact. Some reactions make a compound more toxic or active (bioactivation, e.g. benzo[a]pyrene → carcinogenic epoxide; paracetamol → NAPQI; prodrugs such as codeine → morphine).

Biotransformation occurs in phases: Phase I (functionalisation) → Phase II (conjugation) → Phase III (transport/elimination). Some texts call carrier-mediated uptake into the hepatocyte "phase 0".

Phase I: functionalisation (modification) reactions

Phase I introduces or unmasks a polar functional group (–OH, –NH₂, –SH, –COOH). This makes the molecule slightly more polar and gives a "handle" for phase II. Products may be inactive, active or toxic.

Phase III: transport and elimination (EMPHASIS)

1. Definition and concept

Phase III is the carrier-mediated, energy-dependent export of xenobiotics and, above all, of their phase II conjugates out of the cell: into bile (liver), urine (kidney), the intestinal lumen (gut) or back into blood. Conjugates are charged and large, so they cannot simply diffuse across membranes. Without phase III they would accumulate inside the cell, and some (e.g. glucuronides) would inhibit the conjugating enzymes. Phase III is therefore what actually completes elimination. Some texts also include the further processing of conjugates (mercapturic acid formation) in this phase.

2. The transporters: ATP-binding cassette (ABC) superfamily

The main phase III proteins are ABC transporters. These are integral membrane proteins with transmembrane domains (the channel) and nucleotide-binding domains that hydrolyse ATP to pump substrates against a concentration gradient, which is primary active transport.

Uptake carriers on the sinusoidal side, the organic anion transporting polypeptides (OATPs) and organic cation transporters (OCTs), bring drugs into the hepatocyte. Some texts call this "phase 0". In the kidney, organic anion and cation transporters (OATs/OCTs) of the proximal tubule secrete conjugates into urine.

3. Further metabolism in phase III: the mercapturic acid pathway
Xenobiotic–SG (GSH conjugate)→γ-glutamyl transpeptidase removes Glu→dipeptidase removes Gly→Cysteinyl conjugate→N-acetylation→Mercapturic acid → urine

So the glutathione conjugates formed in phase II are exported (by MRP2) and trimmed in the kidney and bile ducts to N-acetylcysteine (mercapturic acid) conjugates, which are excreted in urine. Paracetamol overdose is a classic example: NAPQI–GSH is excreted as cysteine and mercapturate conjugates.

4. Sites of phase III elimination
  1. Liver → bile → faeces: large conjugates (MW > ~300–500 Da), e.g. bilirubin glucuronide. Some glucuronides are hydrolysed by gut bacterial β-glucuronidase, and the freed drug is reabsorbed: enterohepatic circulation, which prolongs drug action (e.g. oral contraceptives, morphine).
  2. Kidney → urine: small water-soluble conjugates by glomerular filtration plus tubular secretion.
  3. Intestine: P-gp and BCRP in enterocytes pump drugs back into the lumen. This lowers oral bioavailability.
  4. Barrier tissues: the blood–brain barrier, blood–testis barrier and placenta protect the brain, sperm and fetus.
5. Clinical and biochemical importance of phase III
  1. Multidrug resistance in cancer: overexpression of P-gp/MDR1 in tumour cells pumps out many unrelated cytotoxic drugs, so chemotherapy fails.
  2. Dubin–Johnson syndrome: MRP2 defect → conjugated bilirubin cannot enter bile → conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia with dark pigment in the liver.
  3. Drug interactions: P-gp inhibitors (verapamil, quinidine, cyclosporine) raise blood levels of digoxin. Inducers (rifampicin, St John's wort) lower them.
  4. Cholestasis: inhibition of BSEP by some drugs causes drug-induced cholestatic jaundice.
  5. Resistance of microbes and parasites: efflux pumps in bacteria and in Plasmodium (e.g. chloroquine resistance transporters) work on the same principle.

Conclusion

Phase I adds a reactive polar "handle", phase II attaches a large polar group, and phase III pumps the product out of the cell using ATP-driven ABC transporters (P-gp, MRP2, BCRP). Elimination is complete only after phase III. Its failure or overactivity explains Dubin–Johnson syndrome, drug interactions and multidrug resistance.

Understand it (plain English) Think of the cell as a house getting rid of rubbish. Phase I puts a handle on the rubbish (–OH). Phase II packs it into a labelled bin bag (glucuronide, sulphate). Phase III is the refuse truck that carries the bag out of the house through a special door (the ABC pump), paying with ATP. A packed bag that never leaves the house is still in the house. That is why phase III matters.
