What is the role of cytochrome P450 in drug metabolism.

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants What CYP450 is (haem protein, 450 nm, microsomal monooxygenase), the overall reaction, the catalytic cycle diagram, the reactions it carries out with drug examples, the major isoforms (CYP3A4 etc.), induction and inhibition (drug interactions), bioactivation and polymorphism.

Introduction

Cytochrome P450 (CYP) is a superfamily of haem-containing monooxygenases. They are found mainly in the membranes of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum (microsomes) of the liver, and also in the intestine, lungs, kidney and adrenal cortex (where mitochondrial forms make steroids). The name comes from its pigment (P) nature: when its reduced (Fe²⁺) form binds carbon monoxide, it gives an absorption peak at 450 nm. It is the most important enzyme system of phase I drug metabolism. CYP enzymes metabolise most drugs in clinical use, with CYP3A4 alone handling about half.

Overall reaction (mixed-function oxidase)

R–H + O₂ + NADPH + H⁺  →  R–OH + H₂O + NADP⁺

One atom of O₂ is put into the drug (hydroxylation). The other is reduced to water. That is why it is called a monooxygenase or mixed-function oxidase. Electrons come from NADPH via NADPH–cytochrome P450 reductase (a flavoprotein with FAD and FMN), and sometimes from cytochrome b₅.

Steps of the cycle (Fig 224.1)

  1. The drug (RH) binds the oxidised (Fe³⁺) enzyme.
  2. The first electron from NADPH (via NADPH–P450 reductase) reduces Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺.
  3. Molecular O₂ binds to Fe²⁺.
  4. A second electron (from the reductase or cytochrome b₅) forms a peroxo complex.
  5. Two protons are added, the O–O bond splits and one O leaves as H₂O, leaving a highly reactive oxo-iron (FeO³⁺) species.
  6. The oxygen is inserted into the drug, R–OH is released and the enzyme returns to Fe³⁺.

Roles of cytochrome P450 in drug metabolism

  1. Makes drugs more polar (phase I): it adds –OH or unmasks other groups, so the drug can be conjugated in phase II and excreted.
  2. Catalyses many reaction types:
    ReactionExample
    Aliphatic/aromatic hydroxylationPhenobarbitone, phenytoin, benzene → phenol
    N-, O-, S-dealkylationCodeine → morphine (O-demethylation, CYP2D6); diazepam → nordiazepam
    EpoxidationBenzo[a]pyrene → carcinogenic epoxide; aflatoxin B₁
    Oxidative deaminationAmphetamine
    Sulphoxidation, desulphurationChlorpromazine; parathion → paraoxon
    DehalogenationHalothane, CCl₄
  3. Inactivation and termination of drug action for most drugs, which decides their half-life and duration of action.
  4. Activation of prodrugs: codeine → morphine, cyclophosphamide → active alkylating agent, clopidogrel.
  5. Bioactivation to toxic products (a "dark side"): paracetamol → NAPQI (CYP2E1); CCl₄ → CCl₃• free radical (liver necrosis); polycyclic hydrocarbons → carcinogens.
  6. Metabolism of alcohol: CYP2E1 is part of the microsomal ethanol-oxidising system (MEOS). It is induced in chronic drinkers.
  7. Basis of drug–drug interactions:
    • Inducers increase CYP synthesis, so other drugs are cleared faster (risk of therapeutic failure). Examples: phenobarbitone, rifampicin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, chronic alcohol, cigarette smoke (CYP1A2). Rifampicin can make oral contraceptives fail.
    • Inhibitors block CYP, so other drugs accumulate (risk of toxicity). Examples: cimetidine, ketoconazole, erythromycin, ciprofloxacin, grapefruit juice (CYP3A4).
  8. Pharmacogenetics: polymorphisms (e.g. CYP2D6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19) make people poor, extensive or ultra-rapid metabolisers. This explains why the same dose acts differently in different people.

Other (endogenous) roles, briefly

Mitochondrial and microsomal CYPs also make steroid hormones (side-chain cleavage, 21-, 11β- and 17α-hydroxylases), hydroxylate vitamin D (25- and 1α-hydroxylation) and form bile acids (7α-hydroxylase).

Conclusion

Cytochrome P450 is the body's main drug-oxidising enzyme. It decides how fast most drugs are inactivated or activated. Its inducibility, inhibition and genetic variation underlie drug interactions, toxicity and individual variation in drug response.

Understand it (plain English) O₂ is a stable molecule. To make it attack a drug, CYP450 holds O₂ on its iron and "charges" it with two electrons from NADPH. The O–O bond snaps: one oxygen leaves as water, and the other, now very reactive, is stuck onto the drug as –OH. A drug with an –OH handle can then be conjugated and excreted.
Memory trick Inducers = "PC BRAS": Phenytoin, Carbamazepine, Barbiturates, Rifampicin, Alcohol (chronic), Smoking.
Inhibitors = "GKEC" ("Grapefruit Kills Enzyme Completely"): Grapefruit juice, Ketoconazole, Erythromycin, Cimetidine.
How to write this in the exam About 15–20 min. Definition (450 nm, haem, microsomes) → overall equation → cycle diagram → numbered roles (phase I, reactions table, prodrug activation, toxic activation, MEOS, induction/inhibition, polymorphism) → one-line conclusion. The equation and the induction/inhibition examples are sure marks.
Mistakes that cost marks (1) Writing NADH instead of NADPH as the electron donor. (2) Calling it a "dioxygenase". It is a monooxygenase. (3) Saying "450" refers to molecular weight. It is the absorption wavelength (nm) of the CO-bound reduced form. (4) Mixing up inducers and inhibitors in drug-interaction examples.
