What the examiner wants Three equal short notes, each with a definition, classification/examples, and a "medical implications" paragraph (the question says so — don't skip it). Sickle cell (HbA → HbS) is the perfect running example for genotype/phenotype and mutation.

(a) Genotype and phenotype

Genotype is the genetic constitution of an individual — the particular set of alleles present at a gene locus (or in the whole genome), e.g. HbAA, HbAS, HbSS. Phenotype is the observable characteristics (structural, biochemical, physiological, behavioural) that result from the interaction of the genotype with the environment, e.g. normal, sickle cell trait, sickle cell anaemia.

Phenotype = Genotype + Environment (+ their interaction)

  • Alleles: alternative forms of a gene at the same locus. Homozygous = two identical alleles (AA, aa); heterozygous = two different alleles (Aa); hemizygous = only one allele (X-linked genes in males).
  • Dominant allele expresses itself in the heterozygote; recessive is expressed only in the homozygote. In codominance both are expressed (blood group AB; HbAS on electrophoresis).
  • One genotype can give different phenotypes (variable expressivity, incomplete penetrance); different genotypes can give the same phenotype (AA and AO both blood group A; genetic heterogeneity).
Medical implications
  1. Genetic counselling: two AS parents → 25% risk of SS child; genotype testing before marriage is routine in Nigeria.
  2. Phenotype modification by environment: phenylketonuria (genotype) gives mental retardation (phenotype) only if phenylalanine is eaten — a low-phenylalanine diet prevents it. G6PD deficiency shows haemolysis only after oxidant drugs/fava beans.
  3. Pharmacogenetics: genotype decides drug response (slow vs fast acetylators of isoniazid; suxamethonium apnoea with atypical pseudocholinesterase).
  4. Blood transfusion and transplantation depend on ABO, Rh and HLA genotypes; paternity and forensic identification use DNA genotypes.

(b) Mutagens

A mutation is a permanent, heritable change in the nucleotide sequence of DNA. A mutagen is any physical, chemical or biological agent that increases the rate of mutation above the spontaneous rate. Mutagens that cause cancer are carcinogens; most carcinogens are mutagens (basis of the Ames test using Salmonella His⁻ strains).

Types of mutation produced: point mutations (transition/transversion → silent, missense, nonsense), frameshift (insertion/deletion), and large chromosomal changes.

Medical implications
  1. Cancer: aflatoxin (mouldy groundnuts/grains) + HBV → hepatocellular carcinoma — important in Nigeria; smoking → lung cancer; UV → skin cancer, worse in xeroderma pigmentosum (defective nucleotide excision repair) and in albinos.
  2. Inherited disease if germ cells are mutated; teratogenic effects in pregnancy.
  3. Radiation protection for radiographers, avoiding X-rays in early pregnancy.
  4. Useful side: many anticancer drugs are mutagens (alkylating agents, cyclophosphamide) — they kill dividing cells but can cause secondary cancers; mutagens are also used to create useful microbial strains.
  5. Screening of drugs, food additives and cosmetics by the Ames test.

(c) Risks and benefits of gene therapy

Gene therapy is the introduction of a normal (functional) gene into the cells of a patient to correct a genetic defect or to treat a disease. It may be somatic (body cells; not inherited — the only form permitted in humans) or germline (sperm/ova/embryo; inherited — banned on ethical grounds), and ex vivo or in vivo (see Q209).

Medical implications

Gene therapy is suitable mainly for single-gene, recessive disorders where a small amount of normal protein is enough and the target cells are accessible (bone marrow stem cells, liver, airway). Polygenic diseases and dominant-negative mutations are harder to treat.

Conclusion

The genotype is the hidden genetic blueprint and the phenotype its visible expression shaped by environment; mutagens alter this blueprint and cause cancer and inherited disease, while gene therapy tries to repair the blueprint — powerful but still limited by vector safety and ethics.

Understand it (plain English) Genotype = the recipe written in DNA; phenotype = the meal on the plate (also depends on the cook and the kitchen — environment). Mutagens are things that smudge the recipe. Gene therapy is trying to paste a clean copy of the page back in — but the "glue" (usually a virus) can cause trouble.
Memory trick Mutagens: "PCB" — Physical, Chemical, Biological. Chemical sub-types: "BAD AI" — Base analogues, Alkylating, Deaminating, Adduct-formers, Intercalators (intercalators → frameshift). Gene therapy risks: "I-I-E-C" — Immune reaction, Insertional mutagenesis, Ethics, Cost.
