Definition

Forensic biochemistry is the application of biochemical and molecular biological principles and techniques to questions of law — civil and criminal — such as identifying a person, linking a suspect to a crime scene, establishing parentage, determining the cause and time of death and detecting poisons and drugs. ("Forensic" from Latin forum — the court.)

Biological evidence (samples) analysed

Blood and blood stains, semen and vaginal swabs, saliva (cigarette butts, bite marks, cups), hair with roots, skin cells under fingernails, bone, teeth, tissue, urine, vitreous humour, gastric contents and organs (liver, kidney) at autopsy.

A. DNA fingerprinting (DNA profiling)

Principle: Over 99.9% of human DNA is identical between people, but certain non-coding regions are highly polymorphic — tandem repeats whose copy number differs between individuals:

  • VNTRs (minisatellites) — repeat unit 10–100 bp; used by Sir Alec Jeffreys (Leicester, 1984–85), who coined "DNA fingerprinting". First criminal conviction: Colin Pitchfork (UK, 1988).
  • STRs (microsatellites) — repeat unit 2–6 bp; amplified by PCR; now the standard (e.g. the US CODIS core STR loci plus amelogenin for sex).

Except identical twins, the combination of repeat lengths at many loci is unique to each person; one allele at each locus is inherited from each parent.

Steps
  1. Collection of sample with chain of custody; avoid contamination.
  2. DNA extraction (phenol–chloroform, Chelex or silica).
  3. RFLP method: digest with restriction endonuclease (e.g. HinfI) → agarose gel electrophoresis → Southern blotting onto nylon → hybridise with labelled VNTR probe → autoradiography → band pattern.
    PCR-STR method (modern): amplify STR loci with fluorescent primers → capillary electrophoresis → electropherogram (peaks give allele sizes). Works with nanogram or degraded DNA.
  4. Interpretation: compare profiles; a match is reported with a statistical "random match probability" (often 1 in billions).
Other DNA tools
  • Mitochondrial DNA — maternally inherited, many copies per cell; used for hair shafts, old bones, tracing maternal relatives.
  • Y-chromosome STRs — male lineage; useful in sexual assault mixtures.
  • SNP panels — degraded remains, ancestry prediction.

C. Forensic toxicology (detection of poisons and drugs)

Techniques: chromatography (TLC, GC, HPLC), mass spectrometry, spectrophotometry, immunoassays, atomic absorption spectroscopy.

D. Time and cause of death

  • Vitreous humour potassium rises steadily after death — used to estimate post-mortem interval; post-mortem changes in blood glucose, lactate, urea are also studied.
  • Biochemical markers of death mechanisms: troponin (myocardial infarction), tryptase (anaphylaxis), vitreous glucose (diabetic coma), carboxyhaemoglobin (fire deaths — shows victim was alive and breathing).

Applications

  1. Identification of criminals (murder, rape, robbery) and exoneration of the innocent.
  2. Disputed paternity/maternity, immigration and inheritance cases.
  3. Identification of victims of mass disasters (plane crashes, bomb blasts, war graves).
  4. Missing persons; identification of human remains.
  5. Poisoning cases, drink-driving, doping in sports, workplace drug testing.
  6. Wildlife forensics and food adulteration (species identification).

Limitations

  • Contamination and degradation of samples; mixtures from several people.
  • Identical twins share the same nuclear DNA profile.
  • Need for strict chain of custody, accredited laboratories and correct statistics; privacy/ethical issues of DNA databases.

Conclusion

Forensic biochemistry brings the laboratory into the courtroom: DNA profiling identifies individuals with near certainty, biochemical stain tests characterise body fluids, and toxicology explains poisoning — together providing objective evidence for justice.

Understand it (plain English) Your DNA has places where a short "word" is repeated like a stammer — "CATA CATA CATA…". How many times it repeats differs from person to person. Look at 15–20 such places together and the pattern is as unique as a fingerprint. Half of your pattern comes from mum and half from dad — that's why it solves paternity cases.
