Discuss gene mapping and its applications.

Definition

Gene mapping is the determination of the position (locus) of a gene on a chromosome and of the relative distances between genes (or between a gene and DNA markers). The result, a gene map, is like a road map of the genome. The final, highest-resolution map is the complete DNA sequence — achieved for humans by the Human Genome Project (1990–2003).

Principle of linkage mapping

  1. Genes on different chromosomes assort independently (Mendel's 2nd law) — 50% recombinants.
  2. Genes on the same chromosome are linked and tend to be inherited together.
  3. During meiosis I, crossing over between homologous chromatids can separate linked genes. The farther apart two loci are, the more often a crossover occurs between them.
  4. So recombination frequency (recombinant offspring ÷ total offspring × 100) is a measure of distance. First used by Sturtevant (1913) in Drosophila.
  5. In humans, linkage is tested statistically with the LOD score (log of the odds); LOD ≥ 3 (odds 1000:1) = significant linkage; LOD ≤ −2 excludes linkage.

Methods (techniques) of gene mapping

A. Genetic (linkage) mapping
  1. Pedigree / family linkage analysis — follow co-inheritance of a disease with a polymorphic marker through several generations; compute recombination fraction and LOD score.
  2. DNA markers used: RFLP (restriction fragment length polymorphism — first-generation), VNTR/minisatellites, STR/microsatellites (e.g. (CA)n repeats — highly informative) and SNPs (single nucleotide polymorphisms — used in genome-wide association studies).
B. Physical mapping
  1. Cytogenetic mapping — chromosome banding (G-banding); deletions or translocations seen in patients point to the gene's region (e.g. DMD at Xp21 found from X-autosome translocations in affected girls).
  2. Somatic cell hybridisation — human × mouse cells are fused; hybrids randomly lose human chromosomes. If a human protein is produced only when a particular human chromosome is retained, the gene is on that chromosome (synteny). Radiation hybrids refine this.
  3. In situ hybridisation / FISH — a labelled (fluorescent) DNA probe hybridises to its complementary sequence on metaphase chromosomes on a slide; the fluorescent spot shows the gene's band.
  4. Restriction mapping — cutting DNA with several restriction enzymes and measuring fragment sizes to locate cut sites.
  5. Sequence-tagged sites (STS) and contig mapping — overlapping clones (YAC, BAC) are ordered into continuous stretches (contigs).
  6. DNA sequencing — Sanger (dideoxy chain termination) and next-generation sequencing — the ultimate physical map.
  7. Positional cloning — once a disease gene is mapped near markers, chromosome walking/jumping isolates the gene itself (used for CFTR, 1989).

Applications

  1. Identification of disease genes: Huntington disease (chromosome 4, mapped by RFLP linkage in 1983), cystic fibrosis (CFTR, 7q31), Duchenne muscular dystrophy (dystrophin, Xp21), BRCA1/BRCA2 in breast cancer, retinoblastoma (RB1, 13q14).
  2. Prenatal diagnosis and carrier detection: linked markers or the mutation itself are tested in chorionic villus or amniotic cells (e.g. sickle cell, thalassaemia, haemophilia).
  3. Pre-symptomatic (predictive) diagnosis of late-onset disease (Huntington, familial cancers) and genetic counselling.
  4. Understanding disease mechanisms — once found, the gene's protein can be studied (e.g. CFTR as a chloride channel).
  5. Gene therapy and drug design — mapped and cloned genes are the raw material for gene therapy and recombinant drugs.
  6. Pharmacogenomics — mapping of drug-metabolising enzyme variants (e.g. cytochrome P450 polymorphisms) to personalise treatment.
  7. Cancer genetics — mapping of oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes; chromosomal translocations (Philadelphia chromosome t(9;22) in CML).
  8. Forensic science and paternity testing — mapped STR/VNTR loci used for DNA fingerprinting.
  9. Evolution and population studies, and in agriculture — marker-assisted breeding of disease-resistant crops and livestock.

Conclusion

Gene mapping places genes on chromosomes, first approximately by recombination (linkage map, cM) and then precisely by physical methods ending in the DNA sequence. It made possible the discovery of disease genes, molecular diagnosis, genetic counselling and gene therapy.

Understand it (plain English) Think of two passengers sitting in a bus (a chromosome). At each meiosis the bus is "cut and re-joined" somewhere at random. Passengers sitting close together almost always stay in the same half; passengers at opposite ends are often separated. So by counting how often two genes get separated, you know how far apart they sit. Physical mapping is simply measuring the bus seat numbers directly.
