Discuss single cell proteins.

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants Definition (with "dried microbial cells" and "C.L. Wilson 1966"), the 4 groups of organisms with examples, substrates, production steps (with a flowchart), nutritive value, advantages, and the big limitation — high nucleic acid → uric acid/gout — plus how it is reduced.

Definition

Single cell protein (SCP) is the dried biomass (whole cells) or protein extracted from pure or mixed cultures of micro-organisms — bacteria, yeasts, filamentous fungi and algae — grown on cheap substrates and used as a protein source for human food or animal feed. The term was introduced by C.L. Wilson (MIT, 1966); "single cell" because most of these organisms are unicellular (or simple filaments).

Why SCP? (rationale)

  1. World protein shortage, especially in developing countries (kwashiorkor, marasmus).
  2. Microbes grow extremely fast — bacteria double in 20 min–2 h, yeast in 1–3 h, compared with weeks for plants and months for cattle.
  3. They are rich in protein (up to 80% of dry weight in bacteria).
  4. They can be grown on waste materials (molasses, whey, straw, sewage, petroleum by-products), so they also reduce pollution.
  5. Production is independent of land, season and climate and can be done continuously in fermenters.

Production of SCP

  1. Selection of organism — fast-growing, non-pathogenic, non-toxic, high protein, able to use the cheap substrate, easy to harvest.
  2. Substrate preparation — carbon source plus nitrogen (ammonia/urea), phosphate and minerals; sterilised.
  3. Fermentation — batch or continuous culture in an aerated fermenter (air-lift or stirred tank) at controlled pH, temperature and O2; algae are grown in open ponds.
  4. Harvesting — by centrifugation, filtration or flocculation.
  5. Post-harvest treatment — washing, cell disruption, reduction of nucleic acid (heat shock activating endogenous RNase, alkaline hydrolysis, chemical extraction), drying (spray/drum) and packaging.

Nutritive value

  • High-quality protein containing essential amino acids — rich in lysine (good supplement to cereals) but usually limited in methionine (and cysteine).
  • Rich in B-complex vitamins (yeast — thiamine, riboflavin, niacin; algae — β-carotene), minerals and some lipids.

Advantages

  1. Very rapid growth and high yield per unit area and time.
  2. High protein content and good amino acid profile.
  3. Uses waste products — converts pollution into food.
  4. Not dependent on climate, season or arable land; easy genetic improvement of strains.

Disadvantages / limitations

  1. High nucleic acid content (bacteria 8–16%, yeast 6–12% of dry weight). Purines are degraded to uric acid (humans lack uricase) → hyperuricaemia, gout and urate kidney stones. Hence nucleic acid intake from SCP is limited (≈2 g/day) and must be reduced before human use.
  2. Cell walls lower digestibility; may cause indigestion, nausea and allergic (skin) reactions.
  3. Risk of toxins (e.g. mycotoxins in fungi, endotoxin in Gram-negative bacteria), or carcinogenic hydrocarbon residues from petroleum substrates.
  4. Contamination by pathogens; high cost of sterile, aerated fermentation and energy.
  5. Poor taste, colour and texture; low consumer acceptance.

Uses

Human food (Quorn mycoprotein, Spirulina tablets as protein/vitamin supplement, food yeast), animal and poultry feed, fish farming, and as a protein supplement in malnutrition programmes.

Conclusion

SCP is a fast, land-independent protein source that turns wastes into food; its main biochemical drawback is a high nucleic acid content leading to uric acid overload, which is overcome by RNA reduction during processing.

Understand it (plain English) Microbes are tiny protein factories that double every hour. Feed them sugar-cane waste and you harvest kilograms of protein in days. The catch: fast-growing cells are packed with RNA, and when you eat RNA the purines end up as uric acid — the same chemical that causes gout. So the RNA must be destroyed first (heat shock lets the cell's own RNase do it).
