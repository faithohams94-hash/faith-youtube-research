Introduction

The trigeminal nerve is the fifth and the largest cranial nerve. It is a mixed nerve — the chief sensory nerve of the face, scalp (front), teeth, mouth, nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses, and the motor nerve to the muscles of mastication. It is the nerve of the first pharyngeal (mandibular) arch. It is named "trigeminal" (three twins) because of its three divisions: ophthalmic (V1), maxillary (V2) and mandibular (V3).

Functional components

  1. General somatic afferent (GSA) — touch, pain, temperature and proprioception from the face, mouth, teeth, nose, orbit, anterior two-thirds of tongue (general sensation) and the dura of the anterior and middle cranial fossae.
  2. Special visceral (branchial) efferent (SVE) — to the muscles derived from the first arch.

Nuclei (four)

NucleusLocationFunction
1. Mesencephalic nucleusMidbrain, lateral part of central grey (extends into upper pons)Proprioception from muscles of mastication, teeth, TMJ (its cells are unipolar — the only primary sensory neurons inside the CNS)
2. Principal (main/superior) sensory nucleusUpper pons, lateral to motor nucleusTouch and pressure (discriminative)
3. Spinal nucleusLower pons, medulla, down to C2 segment of cord; continuous with substantia gelatinosaPain and temperature
4. Motor nucleusUpper pons, medial to main sensory nucleusMuscles of mastication etc. (SVE)

Second-order fibres from the sensory nuclei cross and form the trigeminal lemniscus, ending in the ventral posteromedial (VPM) nucleus of the thalamus → postcentral gyrus (face area).

Superficial attachment and course of the roots

  1. Attached to the lateral part of the ventral surface of the pons, near its upper border, by a large sensory root and a small motor root (motor root superomedial to the sensory root).
  2. The roots pass forwards from the posterior cranial fossa, across the apex of the petrous temporal bone beneath the attached margin of the tentorium cerebelli and the superior petrosal sinus, into the middle cranial fossa.
  3. The sensory root expands into the trigeminal ganglion; the motor root passes beneath the ganglion and joins the mandibular nerve just below the foramen ovale.

Trigeminal (semilunar / Gasserian) ganglion

  1. Type: sensory ganglion (pseudounipolar cells) — equivalent to a dorsal root ganglion; no synapses.
  2. Situation: in the trigeminal (Meckel's) cave, a pocket of dura mater, lying on the trigeminal impression near the apex of the petrous temporal bone in the middle cranial fossa.
  3. Shape: crescentic, with its convexity directed anterolaterally; the three divisions emerge from the convex border.
  4. Relations: medially — internal carotid artery and posterior part of the cavernous sinus; laterally — middle meningeal artery; superiorly — parahippocampal gyrus; inferiorly — motor root, greater petrosal nerve, apex of petrous temporal bone, foramen lacerum.
  5. Blood supply: internal carotid, middle meningeal and accessory meningeal arteries, and the meningeal branch of the ascending pharyngeal artery.

Motor distribution (V3 only)

Four muscles of mastication — masseter, temporalis, lateral and medial pterygoids — plus mylohyoid, anterior belly of digastric, tensor veli palatini and tensor tympani (all first-arch derivatives).

Sensory distribution on the face

Clinical significance

  1. Trigeminal neuralgia (tic douloureux) — paroxysms of severe, stabbing pain in the area of one division (usually V2 or V3), triggered by touching a "trigger zone".
  2. Corneal reflex — afferent: nasociliary (V1); efferent: facial nerve (orbicularis oculi). Lost early in V1 lesions.
  3. Jaw jerk — afferent and efferent both via V3 (centre: mesencephalic and motor nuclei in the pons); exaggerated in upper motor neuron lesions above the pons.
  4. Lesion of the whole nerve — anaesthesia of the face and anterior scalp, loss of corneal reflex, paralysis of the muscles of mastication; on opening the mouth the jaw deviates to the paralysed side (pushed by the normal lateral pterygoid).
  5. Herpes zoster ophthalmicus — infection of the ganglion involving V1, may cause corneal ulceration.
  6. Referred pain — pain from a carious tooth may be referred to the ear or temple along branches of the same division (e.g. lower molar → ear via auriculotemporal).
  7. Inferior alveolar and lingual nerve blocks are used for dental anaesthesia.

Conclusion

The trigeminal nerve, through four nuclei, one ganglion and three divisions, provides almost all the sensation of the face and the motor supply of the first-arch muscles; its reflexes (corneal, jaw jerk) are routinely tested clinically.

Understand it (plain English) V is the "face nerve for feeling" (VII is the "face nerve for moving"). Like a spinal nerve it has a sensory ganglion; the ganglion's three branches leave the skull through three holes arranged from top to bottom — fissure, round, oval. Only the lowest branch (V3) carries motor fibres, because it serves the jaw, and chewing muscles came from the 1st arch.
