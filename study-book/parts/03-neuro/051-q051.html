Discuss the mid brain at this lower level.

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants "Lower level" = transverse section of the midbrain at the level of the inferior colliculi. Must-haves: the four subdivisions (tectum, tegmentum, substantia nigra, crus cerebri), the inferior colliculus, trochlear nucleus and its unique dorsal course, decussation of superior cerebellar peduncles, the lemnisci, arrangement of fibres in the crus, a labelled diagram, and how this level differs from the superior colliculus level.

Introduction

The midbrain (mesencephalon) is the shortest part of the brainstem (about 2 cm). It connects the pons and cerebellum below with the diencephalon above and passes through the tentorial notch. It is traversed by the cerebral aqueduct (of Sylvius), which divides it into a dorsal tectum (the four colliculi = corpora quadrigemina) and the ventral cerebral peduncles. Each cerebral peduncle is subdivided, from behind forwards, into the tegmentum, the substantia nigra and the crus cerebri (basis pedunculi). Internal structure is studied at two levels — the inferior colliculus (lower) and the superior colliculus (upper).

Transverse section at the level of the inferior colliculi

1. Tectum
  1. Inferior colliculus — a rounded mass of grey matter (nucleus of the inferior colliculus) covered by a thin layer of white matter. It is a relay centre on the auditory pathway: it receives the lateral lemniscus and sends fibres through the inferior brachium to the medial geniculate body. It is also concerned with reflex turning of the head and eyes towards sound (via tectospinal fibres).
2. Tegmentum
  1. Cerebral aqueduct surrounded by the central (periaqueductal) grey matter.
  2. Trochlear nucleus — in the ventral part of the central grey, embedded in the dorsal aspect of the MLF. Its fibres run dorsally and caudally around the central grey, decussate in the superior medullary velum and emerge from the dorsal surface just below the inferior colliculus — the only cranial nerve that emerges dorsally and the only one whose fibres all cross.
  3. Mesencephalic nucleus of the trigeminal nerve — at the lateral margin of the central grey (proprioceptive; its cells are unipolar, like those of a sensory ganglion).
  4. Medial longitudinal fasciculus — ventral to the trochlear nucleus, near the midline; links III, IV, VI and vestibular nuclei for conjugate eye movements.
  5. Decussation of the superior cerebellar peduncles — the most prominent feature in the centre of the tegmentum (dentatorubral and dentatothalamic fibres crossing to the opposite red nucleus and thalamus).
  6. Lemnisci — form a curved band in the lateral tegmentum, from ventromedial to dorsolateral: medial lemniscus → trigeminal lemniscus → spinal lemniscus → lateral lemniscus. The lateral lemniscus ends in the inferior colliculus.
  7. Reticular formation, with the tectospinal and rubrospinal tracts descending in the tegmentum.
3. Substantia nigra

A lamina of grey matter with melanin-containing neurons between tegmentum and crus. It has a dorsal pars compacta (dopaminergic neurons projecting to the corpus striatum — nigrostriatal pathway) and a ventral pars reticularis. It is concerned with muscle tone and smooth initiation of movement.

4. Crus cerebri (basis pedunculi)

Contains descending cortical fibres arranged as: medial one-sixth — frontopontine; intermediate two-thirds — corticospinal and corticonuclear (corticobulbar) fibres (face medially, leg laterally); lateral one-sixth — temporopontine, parietopontine and occipitopontine fibres. Between the two crura lies the interpeduncular fossa.

Blood supply

Branches of the posterior cerebral, superior cerebellar and basilar arteries (paramedian and circumferential branches), plus the posterior choroidal arteries for the tectum.

Clinical significance

  1. Trochlear nerve lesion — paralysis of the superior oblique: diplopia on looking down and in (difficulty walking downstairs); head tilted to the opposite side. A nuclear lesion affects the opposite superior oblique because the fibres cross.
  2. Parkinsonism — degeneration of dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra: rigidity, tremor at rest, slowness of movement.
  3. Aqueductal stenosis — the narrowest part of the CSF pathway; obstruction causes non-communicating hydrocephalus.
  4. Lesion of the crus (Weber's syndrome — at the upper level) gives contralateral hemiplegia.

Conclusion

The lower midbrain is recognised by the inferior colliculus, trochlear nucleus and the decussation of the superior cerebellar peduncles; the trochlear nerve's dorsal crossing course is its classic exam point.

Understand it (plain English) Kris, picture a face looking up at you: the two colliculi are the "eyes" on top, the aqueduct is the "nose", the big X of crossing cerebellar fibres is the "mouth", and the two crura are the "legs" below. At the lower level the "ear" relay (inferior colliculus) is on top and the eye-moving nerve here is IV — the odd one that goes backwards and crosses before leaving.
Memory trick Lemnisci, medial→lateral: "My Tiny Spoon Lifts" = Medial, Trigeminal, Spinal, Lateral. Crus cerebri: "F – CS – TPO", 1/6 – 2/3 – 1/6 (Frontopontine medial, CorticoSpinal middle, Temporo-Parieto-Occipitopontine lateral). Inferior = Ear (hearing), Superior = Seeing.
How to write this in the exam Start with one paragraph on subdivisions of the midbrain, then the diagram, then contents under the four headings Tectum / Tegmentum / Substantia nigra / Crus. Finish with the comparison table — examiners love it because it proves you know both levels.
Mistakes that cost marks Drawing the red nucleus at this level (it is at the superior colliculus level). Saying the trochlear nucleus lies in the floor of the 4th ventricle. Forgetting that the lateral lemniscus ends here. Writing that the trochlear nerve emerges ventrally.
