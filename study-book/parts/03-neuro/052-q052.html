Write on the anatomy of the spino thalamic track.

Definition

The spinothalamic tracts are ascending (sensory) tracts of the spinal cord that arise from cells of the posterior grey column, cross to the opposite side, and ascend through the brainstem to end in the thalamus. They belong to the anterolateral system and carry pain, temperature, crude touch and pressure. There are two: the lateral spinothalamic tract and the anterior (ventral) spinothalamic tract.

Course of the lateral spinothalamic tract (the three neurons)

First-order neuron
  1. Receptors: free nerve endings in skin and deeper tissues (nociceptors and thermoreceptors).
  2. Cell body: pseudounipolar neuron in the dorsal root ganglion.
  3. Central process enters the cord through the lateral division of the dorsal root, runs up or down for 1–2 segments in the dorsolateral tract (of Lissauer), and ends in the posterior grey column.
Second-order neuron
  1. Cell body: in the posterior grey column — substantia gelatinosa (lamina II) and the nucleus proprius (laminae I, IV–V); the main projection cells are in laminae I and V.
  2. Axons cross to the opposite side obliquely (over about one segment) in the anterior white (and grey) commissure, ventral to the central canal.
  3. They ascend in the lateral funiculus of the opposite side as the lateral spinothalamic tract.
  4. Brainstem: in the medulla the tract lies between the olive and the spinal nucleus of the trigeminal nerve; here it is joined by the anterior spinothalamic and spinotectal tracts to form the spinal lemniscus, which ascends through the pons and midbrain lateral to the medial lemniscus. Many fibres give collaterals to the reticular formation.
  5. Termination: the ventral posterolateral (VPL) nucleus of the thalamus (with some fibres to the intralaminar nuclei).
Third-order neuron
  1. Cell body in the VPL nucleus of the thalamus.
  2. Axons pass through the posterior limb of the internal capsule and corona radiata (superior thalamic radiation).
  3. End in the somaesthetic area — the postcentral gyrus (areas 3, 1, 2) of the cerebral cortex.

Anterior spinothalamic tract — differences

First-order fibres enter through the medial division of the dorsal root, ascend 8–10 segments in the posterior white column giving collaterals that synapse in the posterior grey column; second-order axons cross in the anterior white commissure over several segments and ascend in the anterior funiculus. In the brainstem they join the spinal lemniscus and end in the VPL nucleus of the thalamus; third-order neurons go to the postcentral gyrus. Because crude touch is also carried by the posterior column (uncrossed), unilateral damage to this tract causes little loss of touch.

Somatotopic arrangement (lamination)

Because fibres entering at higher levels are added to the medial side of the tract, sacral fibres are most lateral (posterolateral) and cervical fibres most medial (anteromedial) — sequence from lateral to medial: sacral, lumbar, thoracic, cervical. This explains "sacral sparing" in tumours growing from inside the cord.

Functions

  1. Conscious appreciation of pain and temperature (localisation by the cortex; crude awareness at the thalamic level).
  2. Crude touch, pressure, itch and tickle (anterior tract).
  3. Collaterals to the reticular formation contribute to arousal (pain wakes you up).

Clinical significance

  1. Anterolateral cordotomy — cutting the lateral spinothalamic tract relieves intractable pain on the opposite side beginning one or two segments below the cut.
  2. Syringomyelia — a cavity around the central canal destroys the crossing fibres in the anterior white commissure → bilateral loss of pain and temperature in a "cape" over the shoulders and arms, with touch preserved (dissociated sensory loss).
  3. Brown-Séquard syndrome (hemisection of the cord) — loss of pain and temperature on the opposite side below the lesion; loss of position and vibration sense and paralysis on the same side.
  4. Thalamic syndrome — lesion of the VPL nucleus produces contralateral sensory loss with spontaneous, intolerable pain.

Conclusion

The spinothalamic tracts form a crossed, three-neuron pathway from the posterior horn to the thalamus and postcentral gyrus; their early crossing in the cord is the key to the dissociated sensory loss of cord lesions.

Understand it (plain English) Pain and temperature messages "change sides early" — almost as soon as they enter the cord they cross in front of the central canal. Touch from the posterior column crosses late (in the medulla). So a cord lesion on one side kills pain on the other side but position sense on the same side. Three relay stations: DRG → posterior horn → thalamus → cortex.
Memory trick "Spino-thalamic: Swaps sides Soon." Lamination: "SLTC from outside in" — Sacral, Lumbar, Thoracic, Cervical, lateral → medial. Ending: VPL = "Very Painful Limbs" (VPM = face, via trigeminal lemniscus).
How to write this in the exam Draw the pathway first (it earns the most marks), then write the three neurons as numbered points under subheadings. Add the comparison table of lateral vs anterior tracts and two clinical notes (syringomyelia, Brown-Séquard). Always state "crosses in the anterior white commissure" and "ends in VPL nucleus".
Mistakes that cost marks Saying the tract crosses in the medulla (that is the medial lemniscus). Saying it ends directly in the cortex. Putting cervical fibres laterally. Confusing VPL (body) with VPM (face). Forgetting the spinal lemniscus.
