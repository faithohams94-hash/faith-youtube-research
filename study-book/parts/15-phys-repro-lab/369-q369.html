Introduction

The male reproductive system consists of the primary sex organs (testes), the accessory sex organs (epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, prostate, bulbourethral glands, urethra, penis) and the supporting scrotum.

(a) Scrotum

  1. Definition: a cutaneous–fibromuscular pouch hanging below the root of the penis, divided by a septum into two compartments, each containing a testis, epididymis and the lower spermatic cord.
  2. Structure: thin, pigmented, rugose skin with many sweat glands and no subcutaneous fat; deep to it the smooth dartos muscle; the cremaster muscle in the spermatic cord.
  3. Main function — thermoregulation of the testis: spermatogenesis needs a temperature about 2 °C below core body temperature. This is kept by:
    • the testes lying outside the abdominal cavity;
    • thin skin with abundant sweat glands and no fat → heat loss;
    • dartos and cremaster reflexes — in cold they contract, pulling the testes towards the warm body; in heat they relax and the scrotum hangs loose;
    • the pampiniform plexus of veins around the testicular artery acts as a counter-current heat exchanger, cooling arterial blood.
  4. Protects and supports the testes.
  5. Applied: in cryptorchidism (undescended testis) the higher abdominal temperature destroys the germinal epithelium → sterility; varicocele and tight hot clothing also reduce sperm count.

(b) Urethra (male)

  1. Definition: the passage from the internal urethral orifice of the bladder to the external urethral meatus at the tip of the glans; about 18–20 cm long in the male.
  2. Parts: prostatic (≈3 cm; receives the ejaculatory ducts and prostatic ducts), membranous (≈1.5–2 cm; through the urogenital diaphragm, surrounded by the external sphincter), and spongy/penile (≈15 cm; in corpus spongiosum; receives the bulbourethral glands).
  3. Functions:
    • Common passage for urine and semen (urogenital canal).
    • During emission, semen collects in the prostatic urethra; during ejaculation, rhythmic contractions of the bulbospongiosus and ischiocavernosus expel it.
    • The internal sphincter closes during ejaculation, preventing retrograde flow of semen into the bladder and urine into the semen.
    • Mucus of the bulbourethral and urethral (Littre) glands lubricates and neutralises residual acidic urine before ejaculation.
  4. Applied: damage to sympathetic nerves (e.g. diabetes, prostate surgery) → retrograde ejaculation; urethritis (gonococcal) → stricture.

(c) Epididymis

  1. Definition: a comma-shaped organ lying along the posterior border of the testis, formed by a single highly coiled duct about 6 metres long; has a head, body and tail; the tail continues as the vas deferens.
  2. Functions:
    • Maturation of sperm — sperms leaving the testis are non-motile and cannot fertilise. During passage (≈18 hours to several days) they acquire motility and fertilising power under the influence of testosterone-dependent epididymal secretions.
    • Storage of sperm — mainly in the tail (and vas deferens); they remain fertile here for about a month, in an inactive state due to the acidic medium.
    • Secretion of nutrients and maturation factors: glycerylphosphorylcholine, carnitine, sialic acid, glycoproteins, and a decapacitation factor that prevents premature capacitation.
    • Absorption of most of the testicular fluid → concentrates the sperm.
    • Phagocytosis of degenerating, unejaculated sperms.
    • Transport of sperm by peristaltic contraction of its smooth muscle during emission.
  3. Applied: epididymitis or bilateral obstruction (e.g. after tuberculosis/gonorrhoea) → obstructive azoospermia.

(d) Prostate gland

  1. Definition: a fibromuscular glandular organ, walnut-shaped, about 20 g in the adult, lying below the bladder neck and surrounding the prostatic urethra; growth and secretion depend on testosterone (DHT).
  2. Secretion (prostatic fluid): thin, milky, slightly alkaline fluid forming about 20–30% of semen volume. It contains citric acid, calcium, zinc, acid phosphatase, clotting enzyme, profibrinolysin and prostate-specific antigen (PSA).
  3. Functions:
    • Neutralises acidity of the vas deferens fluid and of vaginal secretions (pH 3.5–4) → sperm motility improves (optimal pH 6–6.5 and above).
    • Clotting enzyme converts fibrinogen of seminal vesicle fluid into a coagulum that holds semen in the vagina; fibrinolysin later liquefies it (within 15–30 min), releasing sperm.
    • Citrate and zinc are nutritive/antibacterial; the gland's muscle contracts during emission to expel its secretion.
  4. Applied: benign prostatic hyperplasia in elderly men obstructs urine flow; carcinoma of prostate — raised serum acid phosphatase and PSA are used as markers.

Conclusion

The accessory organs make the testicular sperm usable: the scrotum keeps it cool, the epididymis matures and stores it, the prostate provides a protective alkaline medium, and the urethra delivers it.

Understand it (plain English) Think of a factory line: the testis is the factory (but it must be kept cool — the scrotum is the air-conditioner), the epididymis is the finishing and storage warehouse (sperms "learn to swim" there), the prostate adds an antacid and a gel-then-dissolve system, and the urethra is the delivery pipe shared with urine.
Memory trick Sperm route = SEVEn UP: Seminiferous tubules → Epididymis → Vas deferens → Ejaculatory duct → (nothing) → Urethra → Penis. Prostate fluid = "CAZ-C-F": Citrate, Acid phosphatase, Zinc, Clotting enzyme, Fibrinolysin.

Pick the three you can write best — scrotum (thermoregulation), epididymis and prostate are the easiest to score full marks on.
Mistakes that cost marks Writing only anatomy (relations, blood supply) in a physiology paper — the marks are for functions. Do not say the prostate contributes most of the semen: seminal vesicles give ~60%, prostate ~20–30%.
