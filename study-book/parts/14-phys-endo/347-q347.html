State the chemical classifications of hormones (9mks).

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants Three classes — steroid, protein/peptide (incl. glycoproteins) and amino-acid (tyrosine) derivatives — each with its source glands and several examples, plus the key distinguishing properties (solubility, storage, receptor location). ~3 marks per class.

Introduction

A hormone is a chemical messenger synthesised by an endocrine gland (or specialised cells) and released into the blood, which carries it to distant target cells where it acts at very low concentration. On the basis of chemical nature, Sembulingam groups hormones into three classes:

1. Steroid hormones

  • Synthesised from cholesterol; lipid-soluble.
  • Sources and examples: adrenal cortex — mineralocorticoids (aldosterone, 11-deoxycorticosterone), glucocorticoids (cortisol, corticosterone), adrenal androgens; gonads — testosterone (testis), oestrogen and progesterone (ovary); placenta — oestrogen and progesterone. Vitamin D derivative 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol (calcitriol) is a steroid-like hormone.
  • Properties: not stored (synthesised on demand); transported bound to plasma proteins; cross the cell membrane and act on intracellular (nuclear) receptors → gene expression; slow onset, long action; can be given orally.

2. Protein (peptide) hormones

  • Made of amino acid chains (peptides, polypeptides, proteins; some are glycoproteins); water-soluble.
  • Sources and examples: hypothalamus — releasing/inhibiting hormones (TRH, CRH, GnRH, GHRH, somatostatin); anterior pituitary — GH, prolactin, ACTH; glycoproteins TSH, FSH, LH; posterior pituitary — ADH, oxytocin; pancreas — insulin, glucagon, somatostatin; parathyroid — PTH; thyroid C cells — calcitonin; GI tract — gastrin, secretin, CCK; kidney — erythropoietin, renin (enzyme); placenta — hCG, hCS; heart — atrial natriuretic peptide.
  • Properties: synthesised as larger precursors (pre-prohormone → prohormone → hormone), stored in secretory granules, released by exocytosis; circulate mostly free; act on cell-membrane receptors through second messengers; rapid onset; destroyed by digestive enzymes, so not effective orally.

3. Derivatives of amino acid (tyrosine) — amine hormones

  • Thyroid hormones — thyroxine (T4) and tri-iodothyronine (T3): lipid-soluble, bound to plasma proteins, act on nuclear receptors.
  • Catecholamines — adrenaline, noradrenaline (adrenal medulla), dopamine (hypothalamus): water-soluble, stored in granules, act on membrane receptors.
  • (Melatonin of the pineal is an amine derived from tryptophan.)

Conclusion

Chemically, hormones are steroids, proteins/peptides or tyrosine derivatives; the class predicts how the hormone is stored, transported and where its receptor lies.

Understand it (plain English) Fat-like (steroid) hormones slip through the fatty cell membrane, so their receptors are inside; protein hormones cannot, so they "ring the doorbell" on the membrane. Thyroid hormone is the odd one: made from an amino acid but behaves like a steroid.
Memory trick Steroid glands = "the 3 Cs": Cortex (adrenal), Couples' gonads, Conception organ (placenta). Tyrosine derivatives = "T-CAT": Thyroid + CATecholamines.
How to write this in the exam 9 marks ≈ 12 minutes. Draw the tree first (it alone gets ~4 marks), then three headings with sources, examples and two properties each. Finish with the summary table if time allows.
Mistakes that cost marks Putting thyroxine under proteins or steroids. Forgetting glycoproteins (FSH, LH, TSH, hCG). Listing renin as a hormone without noting it is an enzyme (it acts by forming angiotensin).
