Explain the interactions of hormones giving examples of each (16mks).

Introduction

A target cell is usually exposed to several hormones at the same time. The response it gives depends not only on each hormone but on how the hormones influence one another. Hormone interaction is the modification of the effect (or secretion) of one hormone by another. The main types are:

1. Synergism

When two or more hormones acting on the same target produce the same effect, the combined effect is greater than that of either alone.

  1. Additive synergism — combined effect = sum of the individual effects.
    Example: adrenaline and noradrenaline on heart rate; glucagon and adrenaline on hepatic glycogenolysis.
  2. Potentiation (true synergism) — combined effect is greater than the sum.
    Examples: (i) glucagon + adrenaline + cortisol on blood glucose — together they raise blood glucose far more than the sum of each alone (important in stress); (ii) FSH + testosterone on spermatogenesis; (iii) FSH + LH for full follicular maturation and oestrogen secretion; (iv) GH + thyroid hormone + sex steroids on growth at puberty; (v) oestrogen, progesterone, prolactin, cortisol, GH, insulin act together in mammary development (lactogenesis).

2. Permissiveness (permissive action)

One hormone, which may have little or no effect by itself, must be present for another hormone to exert its full effect. The permissive hormone often increases the number of receptors or the enzymes that the other hormone needs.

  • Cortisol is permissive for the vasoconstrictor and lipolytic actions of catecholamines and for the gluconeogenic and calorigenic effects of glucagon. Without cortisol (Addison's disease) catecholamines cannot maintain blood pressure in stress.
  • Thyroid hormones are permissive for the lipolytic and calorigenic effects of adrenaline (T3 increases β-adrenergic receptors).
  • Oestrogen is permissive for progesterone on the endometrium (oestrogen induces progesterone receptors) and for oxytocin on the uterus (induces oxytocin receptors).
  • Thyroid hormone is permissive for GH action on growth.

3. Antagonism

One hormone opposes or reduces the action of another.

  1. Physiological (functional) antagonism — the two hormones act on different receptors/mechanisms to produce opposite effects:
    • Insulin (↓ blood glucose) vs glucagon, GH, cortisol, adrenaline (↑ blood glucose).
    • PTH (↑ plasma Ca2+) vs calcitonin (↓ plasma Ca2+).
    • Aldosterone (Na+ retention) vs atrial natriuretic peptide (natriuresis).
  2. Antagonism at receptor/secretion level — one hormone reduces the receptors or blocks the action of the other:
    • High oestrogen and progesterone in pregnancy inhibit the milk-secreting action of prolactin on the breast; milk secretion starts only after their fall at delivery.
    • Progesterone reduces oestrogen receptors in the endometrium and lowers uterine sensitivity to oxytocin during pregnancy.
    • Somatostatin inhibits secretion of GH, insulin and glucagon.

4. Tropic (hierarchical) interaction

One hormone controls the secretion of another: hypothalamic releasing hormones → pituitary tropic hormones → target-gland hormones. Examples: TRH → TSH → T3/T4; CRH → ACTH → cortisol; GnRH → LH/FSH → sex steroids.

5. Feedback interaction

  • Negative feedback: target hormone inhibits its controlling hormones — cortisol inhibits CRH and ACTH; thyroxine inhibits TSH and TRH; inhibin inhibits FSH.
  • Positive feedback: rising oestrogen in the late follicular phase stimulates the LH surge → ovulation; oxytocin–cervical stretch in labour.

6. Up- and down-regulation of receptors by another hormone

A hormone can change the number of receptors for another: oestrogen up-regulates LH receptors on granulosa cells (FSH also does this), and progesterone and oxytocin receptors in the uterus; T3 up-regulates β-adrenergic receptors in the heart (explains tachycardia of hyperthyroidism).

Clinical significance

  • Hyperglycaemia of severe stress, infection or trauma results from synergism of glucagon, adrenaline and cortisol.
  • Adrenal insufficiency → loss of the permissive effect of cortisol → circulatory collapse during stress.
  • Hyperthyroid patients are hypersensitive to catecholamines (more β-receptors).

Conclusion

Hormones rarely act alone: synergism, permissiveness, antagonism, tropic control and feedback together fine-tune every endocrine response.

Understand it (plain English) Think of a football team. Synergism: two strikers together score more than each alone. Permissive: the goalkeeper scores no goals, but without him the team cannot play — cortisol is the goalkeeper. Antagonism: two teams pushing the ball in opposite directions — insulin vs glucagon. Tropic: the coach (pituitary) tells the players (target glands) what to do; feedback: players' performance tells the coach whether to push harder.
Memory trick "SPAT-FR" — Synergism, Permissive, Antagonism, Tropic, Feedback, Receptor regulation.
How to write this in the exam 16 marks ≈ 25 minutes. Six headings, each = definition + 2 examples. The bar graph for potentiation is a quick, impressive diagram. Use the words "greater than the sum" (potentiation) and "full effect" (permissive). End with the summary table and clinical notes.
Mistakes that cost marks Defining synergism only as "working together" without the additive/potentiating distinction. Giving permissive examples that are really synergism. Giving only one example per type when the question says "examples of each".
