Explain the anatomical basis of facial nerve palsy.

Introduction

The facial nerve is the seventh cranial nerve, the nerve of the second pharyngeal arch. It is a mixed nerve with a large motor root (to the muscles of facial expression) and a smaller sensory root, the nervus intermedius (taste fibres and parasympathetic secretomotor fibres). Facial palsy is weakness/paralysis of the muscles of facial expression due to a lesion of the nerve or its central connections. Its features depend on where the lesion is.

Key fact: the part of the motor nucleus supplying the upper face (frontalis, upper orbicularis oculi) receives corticonuclear fibres from both cerebral hemispheres, while the part supplying the lower face receives fibres only from the opposite hemisphere.

2. Course of the facial nerve (the basis of localisation)

  1. Intracranial (intrapontine + in posterior cranial fossa): motor fibres loop round the abducent nucleus (forming the facial colliculus) and emerge at the lower border of the pons (cerebellopontine angle) with the nervus intermedius; they enter the internal acoustic meatus together with the vestibulocochlear nerve.
  2. Intrapetrous (in the facial canal): runs laterally above the vestibule to the medial wall of the middle ear, where it bends sharply backwards — the genu, which bears the geniculate ganglion. It then runs backwards in the medial wall of the middle ear above the promontory and oval window, and turns downward in the posterior wall to reach the stylomastoid foramen.
  3. Extracranial: emerges through the stylomastoid foramen, crosses lateral to the base of the styloid process and enters the parotid gland, where it divides into its five terminal branches: temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular and cervical.
Branches
  • From the geniculate ganglion: greater petrosal nerve (secretomotor to lacrimal gland).
  • In the facial canal: nerve to stapedius; chorda tympani (taste, anterior two-thirds of tongue; secretomotor to submandibular and sublingual glands).
  • At exit from stylomastoid foramen: posterior auricular nerve, branch to posterior belly of digastric, branch to stylohyoid.
  • Terminal (in parotid): temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular, cervical.

4. Clinical features of LMN (Bell's) palsy explained

  1. Loss of forehead wrinkles on the affected side — paralysis of frontalis (temporal branch).
  2. Inability to close the eye — paralysis of orbicularis oculi; on attempting closure the eyeball rolls upward (Bell's phenomenon). Tears spill over (epiphora) because the lower lid falls away; the cornea dries → exposure keratitis. Corneal reflex is lost on that side (efferent limb is VII).
  3. Flattening of the nasolabial fold; the angle of the mouth droops and is pulled to the normal side on smiling or showing teeth.
  4. Food collects in the vestibule between cheek and gums — paralysis of buccinator.
  5. Dribbling of saliva from the angle of the mouth; inability to whistle or blow out the cheeks (orbicularis oris).
  6. Hyperacusis if the nerve to stapedius is involved (stapedius normally damps loud sounds).

5. Other anatomical points

  • In the newborn the mastoid process is absent, so the nerve emerging at the stylomastoid foramen is superficial and can be injured by forceps delivery.
  • The nerve in the facial canal lies very close to the middle ear and mastoid — it may be injured in middle ear infections and mastoid operations; fractures of the temporal bone may also damage it.
  • The facial nerve in the parotid lies superficial to the retromandibular vein and external carotid artery — it is at risk in parotid surgery and malignant parotid tumours.

Conclusion

Facial palsy is explained by two anatomical facts: the bilateral cortical supply to the upper face (UMN spares the forehead) and the orderly origin of branches along the course of the nerve (the higher the lesion, the more functions lost besides movement).

Understand it (plain English) The forehead has "two bosses" (both sides of the brain) — so if one boss has a stroke, the other still keeps the forehead working. The lower face has only one boss (the opposite side), so it fails. For nerve injuries, imagine the nerve as a train line with stations: lacrimation leaves first, then stapedius, then taste (chorda). Cut the line early and all stations beyond are lost; cut it at the stylomastoid foramen and only the face muscles are lost.

Mistakes that cost marks (1) Saying UMN palsy affects the same side — it is the opposite lower face. (2) Forgetting that Bell's palsy is at the stylomastoid foramen and therefore has no taste loss in the classic form. (3) Calling the geniculate ganglion a parasympathetic ganglion — it is a sensory ganglion (cell bodies of taste fibres).
