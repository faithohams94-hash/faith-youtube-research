Discuss the actions and nerve supply of the extraocular muscles.

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants List all 7 extraocular muscles (LPS + 4 recti + 2 obliques), origin/insertion in brief, a clean actions table (primary, secondary, tertiary), LR6 SO4 rest 3, the 23° axis explanation, the clinical testing (H) positions, and nerve palsies.

Introduction

The extraocular muscles are the voluntary (striated) muscles of the orbit that move the eyeball and raise the upper eyelid. They are seven: levator palpebrae superioris, four recti — superior, inferior, medial and lateral rectus — and two obliques — superior and inferior oblique. (The orbitalis and the smooth part of the levator are involuntary.)

2. Axes — why the superior and inferior recti and obliques have more than one action

  1. The eyeball moves about three axes through its centre: vertical (adduction/abduction), transverse (elevation/depression) and anteroposterior (intorsion/extorsion — rotation of the upper pole of the cornea medially or laterally).
  2. The visual (optic) axis is anteroposterior, but the orbital axis is directed backwards and medially. The superior and inferior recti run along the orbital axis and make an angle of about 23° with the visual axis; so besides elevation/depression they also adduct and rotate.
  3. The obliques approach the eyeball from the front and medial side (trochlea / maxilla) and insert behind the equator; the superior oblique tendon makes about 51° with the visual axis. Pulling the back of the eye up or down moves the cornea the opposite way, and pulling from medial side abducts.
  4. The medial and lateral recti act along the transverse plane → pure adduction/abduction.

Summary rules: recti (SR, IR) adduct; obliques abduct. "Superiors" (SR, SO) intort; "inferiors" (IR, IO) extort. Elevation of the eye = SR + IO; depression = IR + SO.

4. Clinical testing positions (the "H" test)

To test one muscle in isolation, the eye is first moved so that the muscle's line of pull coincides with the visual axis:

  • In abduction (23°), SR is a pure elevator and IR a pure depressor.
  • In adduction (about 51°), IO is a pure elevator and SO a pure depressor. Thus the superior oblique is tested by asking the patient to look down and in, and the inferior oblique by looking up and in.

5. Nerve supply

  1. Lateral rectus — abducent nerve (VI).
  2. Superior oblique — trochlear nerve (IV), which enters its upper (orbital) surface.
  3. All the rest — oculomotor nerve (III):
    • Superior division → superior rectus and levator palpebrae superioris.
    • Inferior division → medial rectus, inferior rectus and inferior oblique (the branch to inferior oblique also carries parasympathetic fibres to the ciliary ganglion).
  4. Smooth part of levator (Müller's muscle) — sympathetic fibres from the superior cervical ganglion.

The three nerves enter the orbit through the superior orbital fissure: III and VI pass within the common tendinous ring; IV passes above (outside) the ring.

6. Applied anatomy

LesionFeatures
Oculomotor (III) palsyPtosis (paralysed LPS); eye turned down and out (unopposed LR and SO) — lateral squint; diplopia; dilated pupil and loss of accommodation (parasympathetic fibres)
Trochlear (IV) palsyDiplopia on looking down and in — difficulty in going down stairs or reading; patient tilts the head to the opposite side; eye slightly extorted
Abducent (VI) palsyMedial (convergent) squint; diplopia on looking to the affected side; the abducent nerve has a long intracranial course so it is commonly affected in raised intracranial pressure
Horner's syndromePartial ptosis from paralysis of the smooth part of LPS (with miosis)

Squint (strabismus) = deviation of the visual axes from parallelism; paralytic squint follows paralysis of an extraocular muscle and causes diplopia (double vision).

Conclusion

Seven extraocular muscles move the eyeball and lid. Only the medial and lateral recti have a single action; the others act in combination because of the 23° divergence of the orbital and visual axes. The nerve supply is summed up as LR6 SO4, rest 3.

Understand it (plain English) The orbit is a cone pointing backwards and inwards, so the superior rectus pulls the eye from behind-and-medial, not straight back. That is why it lifts the eye but also swings it inward a little. The obliques grab the back of the eye from the front-medial side — pulling the back of the eye up tips the cornea down. That is why the superior oblique looks down.
Memory trick Nerves: LR6 SO4 (rest 3) — like a chemical formula. Actions: "RAD SIN" — Recti ADduct, Superiors INtort (so obliques abduct, inferiors extort). Testing: "Obliques look in, recti look out" before going up/down.
