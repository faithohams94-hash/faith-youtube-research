Here: aspirin. (Paracetamol is worked in Q244.)

Definitions

Xenobiotics (Greek xenos, foreign) are compounds foreign to the body — drugs, food additives, pesticides, pollutants, carcinogens. Biotransformation (detoxification) is the set of enzymatic reactions that convert these usually lipophilic compounds into more polar, water-soluble products that can be excreted in urine or bile. The main site is the liver (smooth endoplasmic reticulum — "microsomal enzymes" — and cytosol); kidney, lung, intestine and skin also contribute. Note: the term "detoxification" is not always accurate — some compounds become more toxic (e.g. paracetamol → NAPQI, benzo[a]pyrene → carcinogenic epoxide).

Phase I — functionalisation

Introduces or unmasks a polar group (–OH, –NH2, –SH, –COOH). Products may be active, inactive or toxic.

  1. Oxidation (most important) — by the cytochrome P450 mono-oxygenase system: RH + O2 + NADPH + H+ → ROH + H2O + NADP+. Examples: hydroxylation of phenobarbitone, benzene → phenol; also non-P450 oxidations — ethanol by alcohol dehydrogenase, amines by monoamine oxidase.
  2. Reduction — nitro and azo reduction: nitrobenzene → aniline; chloral hydrate → trichloroethanol; prontosil → sulphanilamide.
  3. Hydrolysis — esterases and amidases: aspirin → salicylic acid; procaine → p-aminobenzoic acid; lignocaine (amide).

Phase II — conjugation

The Phase I product (or the parent) is joined to an endogenous polar molecule; products are almost always inactive and readily excreted.

Phase III — elimination (transport)

ATP-dependent efflux transporters of the ABC family — P-glycoprotein (MDR-1) and multidrug-resistance-associated proteins (MRP2) — pump conjugates out of hepatocytes into bile and out of renal/intestinal cells. Over-expression of P-gp in tumours causes multidrug resistance to anticancer drugs.

Biotransformation of a named drug — Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid)

  1. Phase I — hydrolysis: aspirin is rapidly hydrolysed by esterases in the gut wall, plasma, liver and RBC to salicylic acid + acetic acid (half-life of aspirin itself only about 15–20 minutes). Salicylic acid is the active anti-inflammatory/analgesic form.
  2. Phase II — glycine conjugation (major): salicylic acid is activated to salicyl-CoA and conjugated with glycine to form salicyluric acid (about three-quarters of a dose). This pathway is saturable — so at high (anti-inflammatory/toxic) doses elimination changes from first-order to zero-order kinetics, explaining salicylate toxicity.
  3. Phase II — glucuronidation: forms salicyl phenolic (ether) glucuronide and acyl (ester) glucuronide — also saturable.
  4. Phase I — minor oxidation: CYP-mediated hydroxylation to gentisic acid (a small fraction).
  5. Excretion: conjugates and some free salicylate leave in urine. Free salicylate excretion increases greatly in alkaline urine (ion trapping) — the biochemical basis of urinary alkalinisation in salicylate poisoning.

Conclusion

Xenobiotic metabolism proceeds through Phase I (functionalisation, mainly CYP450 in the smooth ER), Phase II (conjugation) and Phase III (efflux), converting lipophilic compounds into excretable polar ones. Aspirin illustrates all steps: esterase hydrolysis, saturable glycine/glucuronide conjugation and urinary excretion that rises with alkalinisation.

Understand it (plain English) The kidney can only throw away things that dissolve in water. Phase I "puts a handle" on the oily molecule; Phase II "attaches a big water-loving bag" to the handle; Phase III is the "door-man" who pushes the bag out of the cell.
Memory trick Phase I = "ORH" — Oxidation, Reduction, Hydrolysis. Phase II conjugates = "GAS GMM" — Glucuronide, Acetyl, Sulphate, Glutathione, glycine (aMino acid), Methyl.
