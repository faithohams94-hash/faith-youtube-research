Discuss the developmental origin and consequences of patent ductus arteriosus (PDA).

(3) Why it stays patent (prematurity, rubella, hypoxia). Definition

Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) is the persistence after birth of the ductus arteriosus, the fetal channel that connects the beginning of the left pulmonary artery (at the bifurcation of the pulmonary trunk) to the inferior aspect of the arch of the aorta, just distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery. Normally it closes soon after birth to become the ligamentum arteriosum.

Developmental origin

  1. Six pairs of pharyngeal (aortic) arch arteries develop in sequence, connecting the aortic sac to the paired dorsal aortae; they are never all present together.
  2. The 6th arch artery (pulmonary arch) on each side gives a branch to the developing lung bud.
  3. Right 6th arch: proximal part → proximal part of the right pulmonary artery; distal part (to the dorsal aorta) disappears.
  4. Left 6th arch: proximal part → proximal part of the left pulmonary artery; distal part persists as the ductus arteriosus, connecting the left pulmonary artery with the left dorsal aorta (future arch/descending aorta).
  5. Because the right distal 6th arch disappears, the right recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks round the right subclavian artery (4th arch), whereas the left recurrent laryngeal nerve remains hooked round the ductus (ligamentum) arteriosum.

Function in fetal life

The fetal lungs are collapsed and offer high resistance. Most of the blood leaving the right ventricle (about 90%) is shunted from the pulmonary trunk through the ductus arteriosus into the descending aorta and so to the placenta, bypassing the lungs (see Q85). Its patency is maintained by low oxygen tension and by prostaglandins (PGE2, PGI2) from the placenta and the ductal wall.

Normal closure after birth

Birth: lungs expand, placenta removed→↑ PO2 + ↓ prostaglandins + bradykinin from lungs→Smooth muscle of ductus contracts→Functional closure (within ~1 day)→Intimal proliferation & fibrosis (1–3 months)→Ligamentum arteriosum

Causes of persistence (why the ductus remains patent)

  1. Prematurity — immature ductal smooth muscle responds poorly to oxygen; the commonest setting.
  2. Maternal rubella infection in the first trimester (congenital rubella syndrome).
  3. Neonatal hypoxia — birth asphyxia, respiratory distress syndrome, birth at high altitude.
  4. Female sex (2–3 times more common), genetic factors.
  5. Compensatory persistence with other heart defects (it may be kept open on purpose — see below).

Consequences of PDA

  1. Left-to-right shunt — after birth aortic pressure exceeds pulmonary pressure in both systole and diastole, so blood flows continuously from aorta → pulmonary artery → lungs → LA → LV → aorta again (acyanotic).
  2. Continuous "machinery" murmur (Gibson murmur) heard best in the left 2nd intercostal space / below the left clavicle, with a thrill.
  3. Wide pulse pressure and collapsing (bounding) pulse — diastolic run-off of aortic blood into the low-pressure pulmonary circuit.
  4. Volume overload of LA and LV → left ventricular hypertrophy/dilatation, and in large ducts congestive cardiac failure, poor feeding and growth, recurrent chest infections, especially in preterm babies.
  5. Pulmonary hypertension → Eisenmenger syndrome — the shunt reverses (pulmonary artery → aorta). Since the ductus joins the aorta beyond the left subclavian artery, deoxygenated blood goes only to the lower body: differential cyanosis — cyanosed, clubbed toes with pink fingers.
  6. Infective endarteritis at the ductus.
  7. Surgical relevance — at ligation of a PDA the left recurrent laryngeal nerve may be injured → hoarseness.

A PDA can be life-saving (ductus-dependent lesions)

In pulmonary atresia, severe tetralogy of Fallot, transposition of the great arteries, hypoplastic left heart and preductal coarctation of aorta, the ductus is the only route for pulmonary or systemic blood flow. Here it is deliberately kept open (prostaglandin E1), whereas in a preterm baby with a symptomatic PDA, prostaglandin-synthesis inhibitors (indomethacin, ibuprofen) help it close — preclinical outline only.

Conclusion

The ductus arteriosus is the distal part of the left 6th arch artery. It is vital in fetal life and normally closes after birth under the influence of oxygen and falling prostaglandins. Failure of closure gives a left-to-right aorto-pulmonary shunt with a continuous murmur, which may progress to pulmonary hypertension and differential cyanosis.

Understand it (plain English) Before birth the lungs are "switched off", so the ductus is a bypass pipe that sends blood straight from the pulmonary trunk into the aorta. Oxygen is the "off-switch" for that pipe: at the first breath, oxygen rises, placental prostaglandins vanish, and the muscular pipe squeezes shut. In a premature baby the off-switch is immature — the pipe stays open and now the higher-pressure aorta pours blood backwards into the lungs all the time (systole and diastole) — hence the continuous murmur.
