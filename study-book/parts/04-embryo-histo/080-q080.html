Explain the developmental anomalies arising from abnormal partitioning of the truncus arteriosus.

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants First the normal partition: truncal and conal (bulbar) ridges, neural crest contribution, the spiral aorticopulmonary septum. Then each anomaly as "what went wrong with the septum": failure (persistent truncus), no spiral (transposition), unequal division (tetralogy of Fallot, aortic/pulmonary stenosis/atresia), incomplete fusion (aortopulmonary window). Diagrams of normal vs abnormal outflow tracts earn heavy marks.

Introduction

The truncus arteriosus is the distal part of the outflow tract of the primitive heart tube, continuous proximally with the conus (bulbus) cordis and distally with the aortic sac. During the 5th to 8th weeks it is divided by the aorticopulmonary (spiral) septum into the ascending aorta and the pulmonary trunk. Any fault in the formation, spiralling or position of this septum produces conotruncal anomalies — a major group of cyanotic congenital heart diseases.

Normal partitioning of the truncus arteriosus and conus

  1. Truncal ridges (truncus swellings) — in the 5th week a pair of opposing ridges appears in the wall of the truncus: the right superior and the left inferior truncal ridges. They are formed largely from cardiac neural crest cells migrating from the hindbrain region through pharyngeal arches 3, 4 and 6.
  2. Spiral course — the right superior ridge grows distally and to the left; the left inferior ridge grows distally and to the right. Thus, while growing towards the aortic sac, they twist around each other through about 180°.
  3. Fusion — the ridges fuse in the midline to form the aorticopulmonary septum, dividing the truncus into an aortic channel and a pulmonary channel. Because the septum is spiral, the pulmonary trunk twists around the ascending aorta.
  4. Conus (bulbar) ridges — similar right dorsal and left ventral conus swellings appear in the conus cordis, fuse and become continuous with the truncal septum. The conus forms the infundibulum of the right ventricle and the aortic vestibule of the left ventricle.
  5. Contribution to interventricular septum — the conus septum, with tissue from the endocardial cushions, closes the interventricular foramen and forms the membranous part of the interventricular septum (see Q81).
  6. Semilunar valves — formed from the truncal ridges and two intercalated swellings at the junction of truncus and conus: three cusps each for aortic and pulmonary valves.

Anomalies due to abnormal partitioning

1. Persistent (common) truncus arteriosus — FAILURE of the septum
  • Cause: truncal ridges fail to form or to fuse (deficient neural crest migration).
  • Result: a single large arterial trunk leaves the heart, overriding both ventricles; pulmonary arteries arise from it. It is always accompanied by a VSD, because the conus septum also fails to close the interventricular foramen.
  • Effects: complete mixing of systemic and pulmonary blood → cyanosis; increased pulmonary flow → early heart failure.
2. Transposition of the great arteries (TGA) — septum FAILS TO SPIRAL
  • Cause: conotruncal septum runs straight down instead of spirally.
  • Result: aorta arises from the right ventricle, pulmonary trunk from the left ventricle. Systemic and pulmonary circulations run in parallel.
  • Effects: the commonest cause of cyanotic heart disease in the newborn; life is possible only if there is mixing through an ASD, VSD or patent ductus arteriosus.
3. Tetralogy of Fallot — UNEQUAL division of the conus
  • Cause: anterior (and rightward) displacement of the conotruncal septum, so the pulmonary channel is narrow and the aortic channel wide.
  • Four features: (a) pulmonary (infundibular) stenosis, (b) large VSD (membranous part), (c) overriding aorta arising directly above the VSD, (d) right ventricular hypertrophy (secondary to the stenosis).
  • Effects: commonest cyanotic congenital heart disease beyond infancy; right-to-left shunt; "boot-shaped" heart on X-ray; squatting and cyanotic (hypercyanotic) spells.
4. Valvular stenosis or atresia — unequal division / fused valve cusps
  • Pulmonary valvular stenosis/atresia: pulmonary channel small or valve cusps fused; RV hypoplastic; pulmonary blood flow depends on a patent ductus arteriosus.
  • Aortic valvular stenosis/atresia: aortic cusps thickened/fused; in atresia the left ventricle and ascending aorta are hypoplastic (hypoplastic left heart); systemic flow depends on the ductus.
  • Bicuspid aortic valve — abnormal formation of the valve swellings (common; may calcify later in life).
5. Aortopulmonary septal defect (window) — INCOMPLETE fusion
  • A window between ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk just above the valves, with two separate semilunar valves — left-to-right shunt similar to a large PDA.
6. Double outlet right ventricle
  • Both great arteries arise from the right ventricle with a VSD, from defective conus rotation and septal alignment.

Clinical significance

  1. Conotruncal anomalies are the main cause of cyanotic congenital heart disease (the "5 Ts": Tetralogy, Transposition, Truncus, Tricuspid atresia, Total anomalous pulmonary venous return).
  2. Because cardiac neural crest cells form the truncal ridges, these defects occur with other neural-crest/pharyngeal-arch defects — e.g. DiGeorge syndrome (22q11 deletion): absent thymus and parathyroids, facial anomalies and persistent truncus or tetralogy.
  3. Teratogens: retinoic acid (vitamin A analogues), maternal diabetes, alcohol.
  4. Many of these babies depend on a patent ductus arteriosus for survival in the first days of life (see Q82).

Conclusion

The truncus arteriosus is divided by a spiral, neural-crest-derived septum. Absence, straightening, displacement or incomplete fusion of this septum gives persistent truncus, transposition, tetralogy of Fallot, valvular stenosis/atresia and aortopulmonary window respectively.

Understand it (plain English) Think of the truncus as one hose that must be split into two hoses by a wall that twists half a turn as it grows. No wall = one hose (truncus). Wall but no twist = hoses plugged into the wrong pumps (transposition). Wall pushed too far forward = small pulmonary hose and big aortic hose sitting over the hole in the ventricular wall (Fallot). Most of the "wall-building" cells come from neural crest — that is why DiGeorge babies have these defects.
Memory trick Tetralogy = "PROVe": Pulmonary stenosis, Right ventricular hypertrophy, Overriding aorta, VSD. Cyanotic defects = the 5 Ts — and count on your fingers: 1 trunk (truncus), 2 switched vessels (transposition), 3 = tri-cuspid atresia, 4 = tetra-logy, 5 words = Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Return.

Mistakes that cost marks Forgetting that persistent truncus is always with a VSD. Writing "aorta from LV" in transposition (it is from RV). Listing "ASD" instead of "VSD" in tetralogy. Saying the right ventricular hypertrophy is the primary defect — it is secondary to pulmonary stenosis.
