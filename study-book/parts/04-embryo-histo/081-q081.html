Describe the developmental process of ventricular septation and explain how defects in this process lead to ventricular septal defects (VSDs).

Revision tracker: Read Drew diagram Wrote from memory
What the examiner wants Two parts of the septum with two different origins: muscular part (growth of ventricular walls from the floor) and membranous part (closure of the interventricular foramen by right and left conus/bulbar ridges + endocardial cushion tissue). Then VSD types matched to each failure, with haemodynamic consequences (left-to-right shunt, pansystolic murmur, pulmonary hypertension, Eisenmenger).

Introduction

The primitive ventricle (formed from the bulboventricular part of the heart tube) is divided into right and left ventricles by the interventricular septum between the 4th and 7th weeks. The septum has a large lower muscular part and a small upper membranous part. A ventricular septal defect (VSD) is a persistent opening in this septum; it is the commonest congenital heart defect.

Development of the interventricular septum

A. Muscular part (end of 4th week onward)
  1. The primitive ventricle and the proximal bulbus cordis (future right ventricle) expand; their medial walls come together (apposition) and fuse, forming a crescent-shaped muscular ridge in the floor of the ventricle near the apex.
  2. The ridge grows upward mainly by dilatation of the ventricles on either side (rather than active growth of the septum); myoblasts also add to it.
  3. Its upper free concave edge does not reach the fused endocardial cushions. The gap left is the interventricular foramen, which keeps the two ventricles in communication until the end of the 7th week.
B. Membranous part — closure of the interventricular foramen (7th week)

The interventricular foramen is closed by three sources of tissue:

  1. Right conus (bulbar) ridge,
  2. Left conus (bulbar) ridge — both are the downward continuation of the spiral conotruncal septum,
  3. Fused endocardial cushions (mainly the inferior cushion) — tissue growing along the top of the muscular septum.

These fuse with each other and with the muscular septum to form the thin membranous interventricular septum. After closure, the pulmonary trunk opens into the right ventricle and the aorta into the left ventricle. Part of the membranous septum lies between the left ventricle and the right atrium (atrioventricular part), because the tricuspid valve is attached lower than the mitral valve.

Consequences (clinical implications)

  1. Left-to-right shunt — LV pressure is much higher than RV pressure, so blood flows LV → RV → lungs → LA → LV. The child is initially acyanotic.
  2. Volume overload of pulmonary circulation, left atrium and left ventricle → LA and LV enlargement.
  3. Murmur — harsh pansystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border, often with a thrill. A small defect gives a loud murmur but little disturbance (maladie de Roger).
  4. Large VSD — heart failure, poor feeding and growth, recurrent chest infections in infancy.
  5. Pulmonary hypertension → Eisenmenger syndrome — the shunt reverses (right-to-left) with cyanosis and clubbing.
  6. Infective endocarditis at the jet lesion.
  7. Spontaneous closure — many small muscular and some membranous VSDs close in early childhood.
  8. Associations — tetralogy of Fallot, persistent truncus arteriosus, transposition, Down syndrome; maternal rubella and alcohol.

Conclusion

The interventricular septum is formed by a muscular part from the ventricular floor and a membranous part from the conus ridges and endocardial cushions. Because the membranous part depends on precise fusion of three tissues, it is the commonest site of VSD, whose main effect is a left-to-right shunt.

Understand it (plain English) The muscular wall rises from the floor like a wall being built up from the ground — but it stops short of the ceiling. The last gap near the ceiling is plugged with "putty" from three places (two conus ridges and the cushions). If the putty does not meet properly you get the common membranous VSD; if the brick wall itself has holes you get muscular VSDs.
Memory trick The three closers of the interventricular foramen = "R-L-C": Right conus ridge, Left conus ridge, Cushion. VSD types by position = "MOMI": Membranous, Outlet, Muscular, Inlet — with Membranous as the Most common.
How to write this in the exam Two clear sub-headings (muscular part / membranous part), Fig 81.1, then a table of VSD types with "failure in development", Fig 81.2, then numbered consequences. The words "interventricular foramen", "conus (bulbar) ridges", "endocardial cushions", "left-to-right shunt" and "Eisenmenger" must appear.
Mistakes that cost marks Saying the membranous septum comes from the septum primum (that is atrial). Calling the murmur "continuous" (that is PDA). Forgetting that most VSDs are membranous. Writing that the shunt is right-to-left from the start.
